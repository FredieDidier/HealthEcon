\begin{abstract}
\noindent Brazil's for-profit maternity sector delivers about 80\% of babies by
cesarean. Combining private-insurance claims with national birth records, we
document two facts. First, billed cesarean-to-vaginal fee gaps are not robustly
associated with cesarean use. Second, cesareans concentrate on weekdays and
business hours and fall on weekends and holidays, particularly when performed
before labor. Most weekly variation is common to public and for-profit
establishments; within the same municipality and day, for-profit establishments
show an additional weekend decline of 1.8 percentage points. The evidence is
descriptive and mechanism-based, consistent with supply-side convenience
scheduling rather than a causal estimate of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Cesarean section is the most common major surgery in the world, and its use has
risen far beyond any clinical benchmark. About 21\% of births worldwide are
cesarean deliveries, a share projected to reach nearly 29\% by 2030
\citep{betran2021}, against a World Health Organization reference of 10--15\%,
the range above which population-level gains in maternal and newborn survival
disappear \citep{who2015cesarean}. Overuse is not innocuous: unnecessary
cesareans raise maternal morbidity, respiratory distress and other neonatal
complications, and costs \citep{sandall2018,boerma2018}. Brazil is the frontier
case. Roughly 57\% of all Brazilian births are cesarean, and in the private
insurance sector, which covers about a quarter of the population, the rate
reaches 80\%, the highest documented for any large health system. The excess is
not confined to complicated pregnancies. Among first-time mothers at term with a
single, well-positioned baby who are already in spontaneous labor, the group for
which surgery is hardest to justify on clinical grounds, two thirds of private
weekday deliveries end in a cesarean, against roughly one third in public
hospitals. Understanding what sustains surgical rates of this magnitude in a
modern, well-financed health sector is a first-order question for health policy
well beyond Brazil.

Economic theory offers a natural suspect and a large literature to back it. When
patients cannot judge treatment necessity, physicians act as imperfect agents
and can tilt clinical decisions toward their own interests
\citep{dranove1988,mcguire2000}. The canonical version of this argument is
financial: providers do more of what pays more. \citet{gruber1996physician} show
that obstetricians substitute toward cesareans when fee differentials widen,
\citet{gruber1999physician} confirm the fee margin in Medicaid data, and
\citet{clemens2014} document strong treatment responses to payment changes in
Medicare. The financial channel is not universal, however, even in childbirth:
\citet{grant2009} finds that fee incentives explain little of the cross-sectional
variation in cesarean use, and \citet{alexander2020} shows that paying physicians
to economize can move treatment in unintended ways. Childbirth has become the
laboratory of choice for testing physician agency, precisely because the cesarean
decision is frequent, discretionary, and well measured: \citet{currie2008} show
that
liability pressure distorts delivery mode, \citet{johnson2016} find that
physicians treat informed patients (other physicians) differently from everyone
else, and \citet{card2023} show that being delivered in a hospital with a high
cesarean propensity harms the marginal newborn. Throughout this literature the
operative margin is money. Yet the physician's scarcest input is not billable
revenue but time. A vaginal delivery is a long commitment of uncertain onset and
duration that blocks nights, weekends, and office hours; a scheduled cesarean is
a one-hour appointment at a time of the physician's choosing. Separating this
time-and-convenience channel from the fee channel requires observing actual
physician fees and the exact timing of births in the same setting, a combination
administrative data rarely provide.

This paper separates the two channels at national scale. We combine the universe
of private-insurance hospital claims, filed with the sector's federal regulator
under Brazil's mandatory reporting standard (Troca de Informa\c{c}\~oes na
Sa\'ude Suplementar, TISS), which records the physician fee actually billed for
every delivery from 2015 to 2024, with the universe of official birth records
from the Ministry of Health (Sistema de Informa\c{c}\~oes sobre Nascidos Vivos,
SINASC), 42 million births from 2010 to 2024 that record the exact date and hour
of birth, the clinical group under the World Health Organization's Robson
classification, and an indicator for whether a cesarean was performed before
labor began. The claims measure the financial incentive; the birth records
reveal the time incentive through the calendar. The empirical strategy exploits
two sources of variation: differences in relative fees across municipalities and
over time to test the price channel, and the calendar distribution of observed
births, together with prelabor timing and within-municipality-day sector
comparisons, to characterize the scheduling channel. Three sets of facts
emerge.

First, the observed cross-market fee variation does not support a robust positive
price explanation for the epidemic. The correct financial comparison must
credit the vaginal delivery with the separately billed hourly labor-assistance
fee, which averages 2.7 hours at roughly R\$409 per hour. Once it is counted, a
cesarean pays the physician 10 to 30\% less than a vaginal delivery in Brazil's
largest states, which nonetheless run cesarean rates near 80\%. Across
municipalities and years, and controlling for local income, insurance coverage,
prenatal care, and obstetrician supply, the association between the fee gap and
the cesarean rate is tiny and flips sign across specifications. State-level fee
swings as
large as two log points leave cesarean rates unchanged. Even a 2015 federal
court order directing the national regulator to make insurers pay at least three
times more for vaginal deliveries produced no movement in fees or in cesarean
rates. We find no stable positive association between the billed relative fee and
cesarean use across the observed specifications.

Second, the calendar does. Because the onset of spontaneous labor does not
respect the week, the day on which a birth would naturally fall is as good as
random with respect to medical need, and any systematic weekly pattern in surgery
reveals choices about timing. That delivery dates can be shifted for non-clinical
reasons is well established: births respond to tax and holiday incentives
\citep{dickertconlin1999,gans2009}. Our central estimate compares for-profit with
public establishments \emph{within the same municipality and the same day}, so
that municipality-by-date fixed effects absorb every shock the two sectors share
on a given local day. That comparison leaves a for-profit-specific weekend decline
of 1.8 percentage points and a holiday decline of 2.4 points, and adjusting for the
clinical and maternal composition of each cell attenuates them only to 1.5 and 2.0.
The raw
gradients supply the context: cesarean rates at for-profit establishments fall by
8.3 percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points on national holidays, against 7.0
and 3.5 points at public ones, and half of all cesareans are performed in weekday
business hours, against a 30\% benchmark under uniform timing. Most of the weekly
gradient is thus common to both sectors, and weekly scheduling pervades Brazilian
obstetrics; what distinguishes the for-profit model is the differential on top of it.
The mechanism is visible in the components of the rate: the
aggregate weekend dip is minus 9.7 percentage points for cesareans scheduled
before labor and near zero for cesareans during labor, so it is concentrated in
surgery booked around the physician's calendar. It does not vanish among low-risk first-time
mothers already in spontaneous labor, where the cesarean is intrapartum by
definition; there it reflects weekend decision-making and selection rather than
prelabor scheduling. The pattern is 2.2 points larger in municipalities where
obstetricians are scarce relative to births, and is no stronger for educated
mothers, which points to the supply side rather than to maternal demand for
convenient timing.

Third, convenience has a quantity and a cost. A decomposition over Robson
clinical groups attributes 71\% of the 35 percentage-point private-public gap to
practice style within identical clinical groups, not to differences in case-mix.
Benchmarking each municipality's weekday cesarean propensity against its own
weekend rate places about 50,000 private weekday cesareans per year above that
mechanical benchmark, about one in ten, a count of surgery above the weekend rate
rather than of procedures that scheduling alone caused. Convenience is also
associated with earlier births: private births are 10.9 percentage points more likely to occur
at 37--38 weeks of gestation than public births after controlling for maternal
age, education, and race, and prelabor cesareans mass exactly at those weeks.
Early-term delivery is the margin that the clinical literature links
to higher neonatal respiratory morbidity and intensive-care admission
\citep{tita2009}, and quasi-experimental evidence confirms that non-indicated
cesareans and earlier scheduled births harm newborn health
\citep{costaramon2018,card2023,borra2019}.

We are explicit about what this design delivers. The paper provides structured
descriptive and mechanism evidence on supply-side scheduling, organized around one
tightly controlled quasi-experimental contrast. It does not estimate the causal
effect of scheduling on the total number of cesareans or on neonatal health. The
fee coefficients are conditional associations, not the causal effect of prices; the
weekend and holiday coefficients document the calendar ordering of deliveries
rather than the effect of a randomly assigned weekend, because the observed
delivery date is itself partly chosen; the decomposition is an accounting exercise;
the 50,000 figure is a mechanical benchmark, not a count of procedures scheduling
caused; and the early-term gap is an association between the sector of birth and
gestational age. The within-municipality-day for-profit differential is the one
estimate that approaches a causal reading, and only under the assumption that,
absent the for-profit organizational model, the two sectors' weekend and holiday
changes would have coincided within a municipality. Ownership is not randomly
assigned, so we treat that differential as a tightly controlled contrast rather
than an unconditional causal effect.

A simple model of physician time rationalizes these facts. A physician weighs the
fee gap against a convenience premium, the value of converting an unschedulable,
calendar-blocking delivery into a one-hour appointment; the premium is positive
even where the fee gap is negative, which is what the data show. The model also
explains why fee-based policy levers should have little traction: they act on a
margin the fee data suggest is not operative.

The policy record is consistent with this reading. The 2015 court order to triple
vaginal pay never became an actual fee change, and the flagship quality program,
\emph{Parto Adequado} (``Adequate Childbirth''), tracked a municipal downward
trend that began in 2010, seven years before its dissemination phase, with no break
at adoption, so an observational design cannot attribute the decline to the program
(we develop this in the Supplemental Appendix). If the operative margin is the
organization of obstetric time rather than the fee, the relevant policy class is
organizational: on-call and shift coverage, midwife-led continuity of care, and
hospital scheduling norms, rather than fee schedules.

The paper contributes to three literatures. The first is the economics of
physician agency and supplier-induced demand \citep{mcguire2000,%
gruber1996physician,gruber1999physician,clemens2014}. That literature has
established that providers respond to financial incentives, and the canonical
test is whether treatment intensity tracks fees. We bring a setting where billed
fee differences can be measured directly. Across the observed variation, the
estimates do not provide stable positive support for the financial channel:
cesarean use does not track the fee gap, and it is high precisely
where cesareans pay less. In its place we document a distinct, non-price margin:
supply-side scheduling of surgery around the calendar, the behavior the model
attributes to the opportunity cost of physician time. Our data locate the margin
on the supply side but cannot separate the attending physician's own time from
hospital staffing, operating-room capacity, and scheduling protocols, so we frame
the mechanism as supply-side convenience scheduling rather than as a clean
measurement of the physician's private opportunity cost. This reframes
supplier-induced demand around a non-price margin that the incentive literature
has largely left implicit. In doing so the paper connects to the literature on
physician practice styles and geographic variation in treatment, which finds
that much of the variation in care reflects supply-side practice patterns rather
than patient case-mix \citep{finkelstein2016,molitor2018,cutler2019,%
curriemacleod2016}. We show that in the most extreme cesarean system on record the
operative practice pattern is a scheduling choice, and we quantify it: within
identical clinical groups, practice style accounts for the majority of the
private-public gap.

The second is the economics of childbirth, where the cesarean decision has
become a workhorse for studying physician behavior \citep{currie2008,%
johnson2016,costaramon2018,card2023}. \citet{johnson2016} use information
asymmetry between doctor and patient, and \citet{currie2008} use liability
pressure, to move the delivery-mode margin; \citet{epstein2009} document
persistent physician-specific treatment styles in cesarean use, and
\citet{card2023} trace the consequences of delivery practices for newborn health.
Our contribution is a national empirical anatomy of this system, assembled from the
universe of private claims and birth records, with unusually detailed mechanism
evidence and a within-municipality-day differential between for-profit and public
establishments. Our calendar evidence relates most
directly to \citet{brown1996}, who studies physician demand for leisure in
cesarean decisions; \citet{fabbri2016}, who analyze manipulation of the exact
delivery time; \citet{jacobson2021}, who quantify holiday birth displacement; and
\citet{melo2024}, who study birth-timing manipulation around Carnival in Brazil.
Relative to this literature, our contribution is the national combination of
billed physician fees, public--for-profit calendar contrasts, prelabor timing,
and Robson-group composition.

The third is the design of health policy in middle-income countries. A decade of
Brazilian interventions aimed the fee schedule and mandatory disclosure at the
epidemic and did not move it. We show why: a court order to triple the vaginal fee
never changed relative pay, and the flagship quality program tracked a pre-existing
municipal trend rather than causing it. The evidence redirects attention from
prices to the organization of obstetric time, the on-call coverage and scheduling
norms that determine the convenience premium, as the margin worth regulating.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
describes the private health sector, delivery payment, and the policy
environment. Section~\ref{sec:data} presents the two administrative datasets and
the construction of the analysis files. Section~\ref{sec:notprice} shows that
fees do not explain the epidemic. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} presents the
scheduling evidence and its checks. Section~\ref{sec:cost} quantifies the
epidemic's composition and its gestational-age margin, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Appendix~\ref{sec:model} develops the
model; the Supplemental Appendix reports the policy record and additional
robustness.

\section{Institutional background}\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================

About a quarter of Brazilians hold private health insurance, a parallel system
of insurers and accredited hospitals regulated by the federal
private-health-insurance authority, the National Supplementary Health Agency
(Ag\^encia Nacional de Sa\'ude Suplementar, ANS). The rest rely on the public
single-payer system, the Unified Health System (Sistema \'Unico de Sa\'ude, SUS).
Childbirth is covered by both, but the two sectors deliver very differently.
Figure~\ref{fig:trend} plots the cesarean share of births by sector over time.
In the private sector the rate sits near 80\% throughout, whether measured from
insurance claims or from birth records at for-profit hospitals; in the public
sector it is close to 44\%. Both are far above the 10--15\% range the World
Health Organization uses as a reference.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{../analysis/output/graphs/fig01_csection_trend.pdf}
\caption{Cesarean rate by sector, 2010--2024}\label{fig:trend}
\fignotes{Cesarean share of deliveries. ``Private (TISS claims)'' is the
insurance-financed sector; ``Private/Public (SINASC births)'' classify births by
the legal nature of the establishment (for-profit vs public).}
\end{figure}

The epidemic is also geographically pervasive. Figure~\ref{fig:map} maps the
private cesarean rate by municipality; high rates are not confined to a few
states but blanket the Center-South and much of the country, with the lowest
rates in parts of the North.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../analysis/output/maps/map02_csection_private.pdf}
\caption{Private cesarean rate by municipality, 2020--2024}\label{fig:map}
\fignotes{Cesarean share of private (for-profit establishment) births by
municipality, SINASC 2020--2024. Municipalities with fewer than 100 private
births in the window are left uncolored.}
\end{figure}

Private obstetricians are paid fee-for-service on a procedure schedule. A
delivery generates a fee for the procedure itself, and a vaginal delivery
additionally generates an hourly ``labor assistance'' fee for time spent
attending labor, billed up to six hours. This detail matters for measuring
incentives and is central to Section~\ref{sec:notprice}.

Three policy episodes recur in the paper, and their institutional scope is worth
stating precisely. Resolution RN 368/2015 is a national ANS rule, binding on all
private plans, that created a right to operator-, hospital-, and
physician-specific cesarean rates on request and made the partogram and an
informed-consent term mandatory for elective cesareans. In December 2015 the
Federal Court in S\~ao Paulo, ruling on a civil action by the state prosecutor,
ordered the ANS, a national regulator, to issue rules making plans pay at least
three times more for vaginal than for cesarean delivery; the agency appealed, and
the rule never took effect. \emph{Parto Adequado} (``Adequate Childbirth'') is a
national quality-improvement program run by the ANS together with a large private
hospital and the United States-based Institute for Healthcare Improvement;
adoption is voluntary and at the hospital level, with a 2015--16 pilot and a
2017--2021 dissemination phase of about 113 hospitals in 60 municipalities.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
% =============================================================================

The analysis combines two national administrative universes. The first is the set
of hospital claims that private insurers are required to report to the ANS under
the TISS standard, covering every insurance-financed delivery from 2015 to 2024.
Each claim records the beneficiary's age band and municipality, the provider's
municipality, the diagnosis, and, at the procedure level, the fee billed. We
identify deliveries by their procedure codes, classify each as cesarean or
vaginal, and measure the physician fee for each mode. The second is SINASC, the
Ministry of Health's registry of all live births, with 42 million births from
2010 to 2024. It records the exact date and hour of birth, the Robson clinical
group, gestational age, and an indicator for whether a cesarean was performed
before labor began, and it assigns each birth to an establishment whose sector we
take from the National Registry of Health Establishments (Cadastro Nacional de
Estabelecimentos de Sa\'ude, CNES). The Robson classification is a World Health
Organization standard that sorts every delivery into one of ten mutually
exclusive groups defined by obstetric characteristics such as parity, gestational
age, fetal presentation, and onset of labor; groups~1 and~2 collect first-time
mothers at term with a single, cephalic baby, for whom a cesarean is hardest to
justify. We supplement these sources with obstetrician counts from the CNES
professionals file and with municipal covariates from the Institute for Health
Policy Studies (Instituto de Estudos para Pol\'iticas de Sa\'ude, IEPS), an
independent Brazilian research institute that consolidates administrative records
from many sources, spanning the health system, the economy, and demographics, into
a single municipality-year panel. From it we take gross domestic product per capita,
household income per capita, population, the share of the population with private
health-plan coverage, and the share of births with adequate prenatal care, which
enter the fee regressions as controls for local income, market size, insurance
penetration, and health-system quality.

The two datasets identify two related but distinct populations, and we keep the
distinction throughout. The TISS claims are insurance-financed deliveries, so
their unit is a payer relationship: we call this the private-insurance sector.
SINASC does not record the payer. Its sector flag is the legal nature of the birth
establishment, so its ``for-profit'' category is a provider-ownership contrast, not
an insurance-coverage one, and is not identical to the insurance-financed
population observed in TISS: a SUS-financed birth can occur in a for-profit
hospital, and an insured mother can deliver in a nonprofit one. We therefore
reserve ``private-insurance sector'' for TISS results and describe SINASC results
as comparing \emph{for-profit} with public establishments.

From these sources we build two files. The first is a municipality-year panel of
private deliveries from the claims, with the cesarean rate, the two fees, and
municipal covariates, used for the fee analysis. The second is a
municipality-by-date file of births by sector from the registry, used for the
timing analysis. Table~\ref{tab:descriptives} reports summary statistics for the
municipality-year panel. Appendix~\ref{app:data} documents the construction and
defines the main variables, and the complete codebook is distributed with the
replication package.

\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:strategy}
% =============================================================================

A simple framework organizes the two designs; Appendix~\ref{sec:model} develops
it formally. A physician choosing between a vaginal delivery and a cesarean weighs,
alongside the fee difference $f_c - f_v$, a convenience premium $\Pi$: the value of
converting an unschedulable, long, calendar-blocking event into a one-hour
procedure at a time of her choosing. She performs a prelabor cesarean when
$(f_c - f_v) + \Pi$ exceeds the patient's net clinical benefit of a vaginal
delivery. Because the premium is positive even where the fee gap is negative, the
framework predicts that cesarean use need not track fees (the price design below),
that prelabor cesareans bunch in low-cost weekday business hours and fall on
weekends and holidays (the time design), that the dip is larger where physician
time is scarcer, and that scheduling shifts births earlier. We isolate the
financial channel from the time channel with two complementary designs, one for
each, and close with a decomposition that sizes the result.

The price channel is estimated by relating the private cesarean rate to the
relative price of a cesarean across municipality-years. For municipality $m$ in
year $t$,
\begin{equation}
\text{CesareanRate}_{mt} = \beta\,\text{LogFeeGap}_{mt}
  + X_{mt}'\gamma + \mu_m + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{mt},
\label{eq:feegap}
\end{equation}
where $\text{LogFeeGap}_{mt} = \log\!\left(f^{c}_{mt}/f^{v}_{mt}\right)$ is the log
ratio of the mean cesarean fee to the mean \emph{economic} vaginal fee, defined as
the vaginal delivery fee plus the separately billed hourly labor-assistance fee;
$X_{mt}$ collects the municipal covariates (log GDP per capita, private health-plan
coverage, adequate prenatal care, and obstetrician density); $\mu_m$ and $\delta_t$
are municipality (or state) and year fixed effects; and observations are weighted
by the number of private deliveries. The coefficient of interest is $\beta$, the
response of the cesarean rate to the relative fee, which the supplier-induced-demand
hypothesis predicts to be positive. Standard errors are clustered on the
fixed-effect geography. Because within-municipality fee variation may be
confounded, we complement Equation~\eqref{eq:feegap} with a first-difference
falsification that asks whether year-to-year swings in the state fee gap move the
cesarean rate.

The time channel rests on a contrast between two dates. The natural onset of
spontaneous labor does not respect the calendar, but the observed delivery date
can be moved through induction or prelabor cesarean scheduling. Systematic weekly
patterns in the mode of delivery therefore document calendar sorting of
deliveries, that is, choices about when births occur, rather than differences in
medical need among the mothers whose labor begins on different days.
On municipality-by-date cells within each sector we estimate
\begin{equation}
\text{CesareanShare}_{mdt} = \beta_1\,\text{Weekend}_{d}
  + \beta_2\,\text{Holiday}_{d} + \beta_3\,\text{Eve}_{d}
  + \mu_m + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{mdt},
\label{eq:scheduling}
\end{equation}
where $\text{CesareanShare}_{mdt}$ is the share of births delivered by cesarean in
municipality $m$ on date $d$ in year $t$; $\text{Weekend}$, $\text{Holiday}$, and
$\text{Eve}$ indicate weekends, national holidays, and the eve of a rest day;
$\mu_m$ and $\delta_t$ are municipality and year fixed effects; cells are weighted
by births; and standard errors are two-way clustered by municipality and date. The
coefficients $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ measure the weekend and holiday dips in the
cesarean rate. Because the observed delivery date can itself be chosen for a
scheduled cesarean, we read $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ as calendar sorting of
cesarean deliveries, that is, as scheduling patterns, rather than as the causal
effect of an as-good-as-random weekend assignment on medical need. We estimate
Equation~\eqref{eq:scheduling} separately
for the private and public sectors and, in extensions, within low-risk Robson
groups, within a clinically clean sample, and separately for cesareans scheduled
before as opposed to during labor. Birth-level regressions of gestational and
neonatal outcomes on a for-profit-establishment indicator take the same two-way
fixed-effects form with maternal controls.

Equation~\eqref{eq:scheduling} measures each sector's own calendar gradient, but
those gradients also absorb whatever the two sectors share: local staffing,
operating-room capacity, municipal holidays, and weather. Our central timing
estimate is therefore the difference between them, taken within a municipality-day.
Pooling both sectors over municipality-by-date-by-sector cells $(m,d,s)$, we
estimate
\begin{multline}
\text{CesareanShare}_{mds} = \gamma_1\,\text{ForProfit}_{s}\!\times\!\text{Weekend}_{d}
  + \gamma_2\,\text{ForProfit}_{s}\!\times\!\text{Holiday}_{d} \\
  + \gamma_3\,\text{ForProfit}_{s}\!\times\!\text{Eve}_{d}
  + \lambda_{md} + \phi_{ms} + \varepsilon_{mds},
\label{eq:gradient}
\end{multline}
where $\lambda_{md}$ are municipality-by-date fixed effects and $\phi_{ms}$
municipality-by-sector fixed effects, cells are weighted by births, and standard
errors are two-way clustered by municipality and date. The municipality-by-date
effects absorb every shock common to both sectors on a given local day, and the
municipality-by-sector effects absorb each sector's level within the municipality,
so $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ are the calendar gradients specific to the for-profit
organizational model. They carry a causal interpretation only under the assumption
that, absent that model, the two sectors would have experienced the same weekend
and holiday changes within the municipality,
$E[\Delta Y^{0}_{md}\mid \text{for-profit}] = E[\Delta Y^{0}_{md}\mid \text{public}]$.
Because neither establishment ownership nor the sorting of mothers across sectors is
randomly assigned, that assumption fails if case-mix moves differentially across
sectors and days of the week. We probe it by re-estimating
Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient} with each cell's own clinical and maternal composition
as controls, namely the cell shares of Robson group, maternal age band, education,
race, and parity. We therefore describe $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ as a tightly
controlled for-profit-versus-public differential rather than an unconditional causal
effect.

Two features of the estimand deserve emphasis. First, the coefficients in
Equations~\eqref{eq:scheduling} and~\eqref{eq:gradient} are shares, not counts, so
they describe how the mode of delivery is ordered across the calendar and not how
many cesareans are performed in total. Second, the design does not satisfy the
stable-unit-treatment-value assumption in the sense a treatment-effect design would
require. A cesarean avoided on Sunday is largely a cesarean performed on Friday or
Monday, and a mother turned away from one establishment may deliver at another, so
weekend and weekday cells are not independent units. Displacement across days is the
mechanism we are documenting rather than a nuisance, and the eve-of-rest-day
coefficient is a direct test for it. But it means the weekend coefficient is a
net-of-displacement contrast between adjacent days, and neither it nor the
counterfactual exercise of Section~\ref{sec:cost} identifies the number of
cesareans that scheduling causes.

The identifying assumption for the time design is that mothers who give birth on
weekends do not differ from weekday mothers in ways correlated with the cesarean
decision. The natural concern is selection: if weekend births were systematically
lower-risk, a lower weekend cesarean rate could reflect case-mix rather than
scheduling. Four features of the design address this. The dip survives within
low-risk Robson groups and within a clinically clean sample with no schedulable
indication, where case-mix is held nearly fixed; the aggregate for-profit-sector dip
is concentrated in prelabor cesareans, the only ones scheduling can move, although
sizable intrapartum weekend differences remain in Robson group 1; a composition
check shows that weekend newborns are, if anything, slightly higher-risk, the
opposite of what selection toward healthy weekend births would imply; and a
descriptive ranking across all twenty-one two-day pairs places the actual weekend
first, although the pairs are not exchangeable under a known assignment mechanism.
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} and the Supplemental Appendix report each of these.

Finally, we gauge how much of the private-public gap reflects practice style rather
than case-mix with a Kitagawa decomposition over the ten Robson groups, and we
benchmark each municipality's weekday cesarean propensity against its own weekend
rate to calculate the number of weekday cesareans above a mechanical
own-municipality weekend benchmark.

\section{Fees and the cesarean rate}\label{sec:notprice}
% =============================================================================

The natural explanation for a high cesarean rate is that cesareans pay more. The
correct comparison, however, must credit the vaginal delivery with the hourly
labor-assistance fee it generates, which averages 2.7 hours at about R\$409 per
hour. Once it is counted, the \emph{economic} cesarean-minus-vaginal fee gap is
negative in Brazil's largest states, by 10 to 30 percent, even though those
states run cesarean rates near 80 percent. The raw correlation already points
away from a positive price explanation.

Table~\ref{tab:not_a_price} makes this precise by estimating
Equation~\eqref{eq:feegap}: it regresses the private cesarean rate on the log
economic fee gap across municipality-years, weighting by deliveries and adding
state or municipality fixed effects and controls for income, plan coverage,
prenatal care, and obstetrician density. The coefficient is small and its sign
flips with the fixed-effect scheme: with the full controls it is a positive 0.017
under state fixed effects and a negative 0.014 under municipality fixed effects,
neither significant, and in the specifications without controls it stays small but
again switches sign. A fee gap that swings by a full log point moves the cesarean
rate by less than two percentage points, with an interval that includes zero.

\input{../analysis/output/tables/tab02_not_a_price.tex}

Fees do move over time, and Table~\ref{tab:fee_shock} exploits that variation
directly. State-level fee gaps swing by as much as two log points from one year
to the next, yet cesarean rates do not follow. In first differences the
coefficient is small and wrong-signed; once the twenty-two states are treated as
the clusters they are, a wild-cluster bootstrap cannot distinguish it from zero
($p=0.21$), and in levels with state and year fixed effects it is likewise
insignificant. Consistent with all of this, the 2015 court order to triple
vaginal pay produced no jump in the fee series and no change in the cesarean
rate. Taken together, the associations range from negative to positive and are
sensitive to the fixed-effect and aggregation scheme; the observed cross-market
fee variation does not support a robust positive price explanation for the
epidemic.

\section{Scheduling and the timing of cesareans}\label{sec:mechanism}
% =============================================================================

If not a stable positive fee relationship, the data point to the calendar. The
natural onset of spontaneous labor does not respect the week, but the observed
delivery date can be changed through induction or prelabor cesarean scheduling.
Weekly differences in the observed cesarean rate therefore document calendar
sorting of deliveries. They are consistent with choices about timing, but they do
not estimate the causal effect of an as-good-as-random weekend assignment.
Figure~\ref{fig:dow} plots the cesarean rate by day of
the week and sector. At for-profit establishments the rate is flat at about 80 percent
Monday through Friday and drops sharply on Saturday and Sunday. Public
establishments show the same shape at a lower level, with the nonprofit sector in
between.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../analysis/output/graphs/fig02_dow_cesarean.pdf}
\caption{Cesarean rate by day of week and sector}\label{fig:dow}
\fignotes{SINASC 2010--2024, all births, by establishment sector.}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:scheduling} estimates the dips in municipality-by-date cells with
municipality and year fixed effects, two-way clustered by municipality and date.
For-profit cesarean rates fall by 8.3 percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points
on national holidays, against public dips of 7.0 and 3.5 points. Most of the
weekend dip is thus common to both sectors, which is itself informative: weekly
scheduling pervades Brazilian obstetrics. The result that most sharply separates the
for-profit model from the public one is the difference \emph{within the same
municipality and day}. Estimating Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient}, which pools the two
sectors with municipality-by-date and municipality-by-sector fixed effects so that
the municipality-by-date effects absorb every shock common to both sectors on a
given local day, leaves a for-profit-specific differential of a further 1.8
percentage points on weekends and 2.4 points on holidays, both statistically
significant (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_did}). This local contrast is our central timing
estimate: it holds geography, seasonality, and municipal holidays fixed and compares
only the two ownership types on the same day. It is a tightly controlled
differential rather than an unconditional causal effect, because ownership is not
randomly assigned and mothers sort across sectors. The natural threat is that
case-mix moves differentially across sectors from weekday to weekend.
Table~\ref{tab:pooled_did_composition} adds each cell's own composition in two
steps. Adjustment for predetermined maternal composition, namely the shares of
births in each maternal age band, education category, race, and parity category,
modestly changes the differential: the weekend coefficient is unchanged at 1.8
percentage points and the holiday coefficient moves from 2.4 to 2.3. Adding
Robson-group shares yields a further clinically standardized comparison, 1.5 points
on weekends and 2.0 on holidays, although Robson status may itself respond to
scheduling, since the classification encodes labor onset, gestational age, and the
mode of labor onset, and it is therefore not treated as a purely predetermined
control. All six coefficients remain significant at the one percent level. The
pattern is consistent with a for-profit-specific supply-side scheduling margin
rather than only calendar shocks common to both sectors. It does not distinguish
physician convenience from ownership-specific staffing, operating-room capacity,
or scheduling protocols. Figure~\ref{fig:hours} shows that for-profit cesareans are absent
overnight and spike between 8 and 11 in the morning, the operating-room block,
while vaginal births are nearly uniform around the clock. Half of all cesareans
occur in weekday business hours, against a benchmark of 29.8 percent under
uniform timing, and only about a third of vaginal births do.

The components of the cesarean rate locate the mechanism.
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks} splits it into cesareans performed before labor
began and cesareans performed during labor. At for-profit establishments the aggregate
weekend dip is 9.7 percentage points for prelabor cesareans and a positive 1.7
points for in-labor cesareans. Scheduling operates on prelabor surgery, and that
is where the aggregate dip is concentrated; the small positive in-labor term is
displacement, as women who are not scheduled go into labor and some convert. This
split is not an artifact of the timing indicator, which is missing for about 16
percent of cesareans at a rate that is essentially identical on weekdays and
weekends. Daily counts, in Figure~\ref{fig:daily_counts},
show cesarean volumes collapsing on weekends while vaginal volumes barely move,
which rules out a compositional change in who is admitted.

The pattern is not confined to complicated pregnancies. Figure~\ref{fig:robson_dow}
and Table~\ref{tab:robson} restrict attention to Robson groups 1 and 2, first-time
mothers at term with a single cephalic baby, the births for which a cesarean is
hardest to justify. The private weekday rate still exceeds 70 percent and the
weekend dip is 7.4 percentage points, against 5.0 in the public sector. Among
Robson group 1 alone, first-time mothers in spontaneous labor, the private dip is
still 6.5 points. Because these women are in labor by definition, this particular
dip cannot come from prelabor scheduling; it reflects intrapartum decisions and
selection into who delivers on a weekend, and it shows that the weekly calendar
reaches even the births that scheduling cannot directly move. The Supplemental
Appendix adds a descriptive placebo: across all twenty-one ways
of choosing two days of the week as ``rest days,'' the true weekend produces the
largest dip of the twenty-one. Because the days of the week are not exchangeable
under a known assignment mechanism, we read this as a descriptive ranking rather
than an exact randomization-inference p-value.

Two heterogeneity tests, in Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity}, connect the pattern to
the supply side rather than to patient demand. The weekend dip is 2.2 percentage
points larger in municipalities where obstetricians are scarce relative to
births, consistent with a higher opportunity cost of unschedulable time, though
scarcity of obstetricians and of hospital capacity move together and we cannot
fully separate them. The education split points the same way. The implied weekend
dip is 21.1 percentage points for mothers with fewer than eight years of schooling
and only 6.7 points for more-educated mothers, so the dip is if anything smaller
where the mother is more educated. This is hard to square with a story in which
mothers themselves demand convenient timing, and points instead to the supply
side, consistent with evidence that physician beliefs and practice patterns,
rather than patient preferences, drive much of the variation in treatment
intensity \citep{cutler2019}.
Physician-owned cooperative insurers use cesareans slightly
more than ordinary insurer-run plans conditional on case-mix
(Table~\ref{tab:modality}), though the difference is not significant. Because this
section and Section~\ref{sec:cost} each report several tests, the Supplemental
Appendix groups the hypotheses into pre-specified families and reports
Holm-Bonferroni adjusted $p$-values and Benjamini-Hochberg $q$-values within each;
no conclusion in the paper depends on the adjustment.

\section{Gestational-age and billed-amount margins}\label{sec:cost}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{The gestational-age margin.} Scheduling a cesarean means performing it
before labor, and to be sure of preceding labor the physician must book it before
the natural rise in the delivery hazard around week 39. The mechanical consequence
is a shift of births to earlier gestational ages. Figure~\ref{fig:gestation} shows
that for-profit births
mass at 37 and 38 weeks where public births mass at 39 and 40, and the shift is
concentrated among prelabor cesareans (Figure~\ref{fig:gestation_timing}).
Table~\ref{tab:health} estimates that for-profit births are 10.9 percentage points
more likely to be early-term (37--38 weeks) than public births, after controlling
for maternal age, education, and race with municipality and year fixed effects.
The estimate is associational. Sector and delivery mode are chosen, and the
maternal controls cannot absorb differences in pregnancy risk, assisted
reproduction, prior cesarean, or the measurement of gestational age, so the
early-term coefficient is a descriptive sector difference and is not interpreted as
the causal effect of prelabor scheduling. Early-term delivery is the margin the
clinical literature links to higher neonatal respiratory morbidity and
intensive-care use \citep{tita2009}, and quasi-experimental work confirms that
non-indicated and early cesareans harm newborn health \citep{costaramon2018,card2023}.
For-profit births show lower
low-birthweight and low-Apgar rates, which reflects the better resources and
healthier mothers of that sector; we report these honestly, and the descriptive
health margin associated with the for-profit delivery system is a shift toward
early-term birth.

This shift is not costless. \citet{tita2009} find that elective delivery at
37--38 weeks, rather than at 39, raises the rate of adverse neonatal outcomes,
including respiratory morbidity and intensive-care admission. We deliberately do
not convert our associational sector gap into a count of harmed newborns: the
mapping from a chosen sector to a causal harm requires a design we do not have.
The direction, however, is what both the clinical literature \citep{tita2009} and
quasi-experimental evidence on non-indicated and early cesareans
\citep{costaramon2018,card2023} would predict, and it locates the cost of the
convenience margin in earlier, riskier births rather than in spending.

\paragraph{The billed-amount margin.} The convenience is not reflected in billed
amounts either. Table~\ref{tab:cost} sums every item billed under each delivery
hospitalization, and a cesarean and a vaginal delivery generate similar gross
billed amounts: the mean billed amounts are within a few reais and the median
cesarean bills about five percent more. These are charged amounts, not the
negotiated prices ultimately paid. Cesareans are therefore not obviously more
lucrative to bill; consistent with the fee analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:notprice}, the epidemic's margin lies in earlier, riskier births
rather than in higher billed amounts per delivery.

\input{../analysis/output/tables/tab12_cost.tex}

\paragraph{The size of the margin.} Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} decomposes the
35 percentage-point private-public gap over Robson clinical groups. Only 29
percent reflects differences in case-mix; the remaining 71 percent is
within-group practice style, the same women managed differently. That most of the
gap is practice style rather than patient composition echoes the finding, in the
literature on geographic variation in health care, that supply-side practice
patterns dominate patient case-mix \citep{finkelstein2016,molitor2018}.
Benchmarking
each municipality's weekday cesarean propensity against its own weekend rate
places about 50,000 private weekday cesareans per year above that mechanical
benchmark, roughly one in ten. This counts cesareans above the own-municipality
weekend rate; it does not separate procedures that would not have occurred from
those merely shifted off the weekend, and the weekend rate itself need not be the
clinical optimum. Consistent with the
picture that most for-profit cesareans are elective, about 88 to 91 percent carry no
recorded clinical indication in the claims, only a delivery-outcome code
(Supplemental Appendix).

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

Brazil's for-profit maternity sector performs cesareans at a rate unmatched by any
large health system, and the standard economic explanation does not fit. Measured
with the actual fees, cesareans do not pay more than vaginal deliveries where the
epidemic is worst, and we find no stable positive association between the relative
fee and cesarean use. What the data reveal instead is a scheduling pattern. The
cesarean converts an unschedulable, long, calendar-blocking event into a one-hour
appointment, and its use bears every fingerprint of that logic: it clusters on
weekdays and in business hours, it dips on weekends and holidays, the aggregate
dip is concentrated in surgery booked before labor while sizable intrapartum
differences remain among the lowest-risk mothers, and it is larger where
obstetrician time is scarcest. Within the same municipality and the same day,
for-profit establishments cut cesareans by a further 1.8 percentage points on
weekends than public ones do. The convenience is associated with earlier births.
These patterns are consistent with supply-side convenience scheduling, though our
data cannot isolate the attending physician's own time from hospital staffing,
operating-room capacity, and scheduling protocols.

What the paper offers is structured descriptive and mechanism evidence organized
around one tightly controlled quasi-experimental contrast, not a causal estimate of
scheduling. The fee coefficients are conditional associations; the calendar
coefficients describe how deliveries are ordered across the week; the decomposition
is an accounting identity; the early-term gap is an association between the sector
of birth and gestational age; and the within-municipality-day for-profit
differential is causal only under an assumption about ownership that no
randomization supports. The contribution lies in the breadth and internal
consistency of the anatomy rather than in a single identified parameter.

The reading carries a policy lesson. If the operative margin is the organization of
obstetric time rather than the fee, the levers most likely to move the epidemic are
organizational rather than financial, and three directions follow from the
analysis. The first acts on the convenience premium directly: detaching the
delivery from a single attending physician's calendar, through laborist or
shift-based on-call coverage and midwife-led continuity of care, socializes the
cost of unschedulable time that the premium capitalizes. The second changes how
care is organized rather than how it is priced. Because most of the private-public
gap is supply-side practice style rather than case-mix, and because practice
styles travel with providers rather than with patients
\citep{finkelstein2016,molitor2018,cutler2019}, salaried or vertically integrated
arrangements in place of the private-office fee-for-service model act on the
margin that actually varies, whereas relative-price reforms do not
\citep{clemens2014,alexander2020}. The third ties regulation to gestational and
delivery outcomes rather than to prices, since the harm the epidemic produces is
concentrated in the delivery practices themselves \citep{card2023}. The observed
fee variation does not reveal a stable positive response, while the calendar
evidence points to the organization of obstetric time as a promising policy
margin.

How far these conclusions travel depends on which part of the setting one carries
along. Three features of Brazil's private sector are extreme rather than typical:
an 80\% cesarean rate that leaves little room for the margin to move further, a
private-office fee-for-service model in which one obstetrician follows a patient
through pregnancy and expects to attend the delivery personally, and a fee schedule
in which the vaginal delivery is the better-paid procedure once labor assistance is
counted. Our magnitudes are specific to that configuration, and the negative fee gap
in particular is unusual enough that the price null should not be read as a general
statement about how obstetricians respond to prices. What is portable is the
mechanism and the diagnostic. Wherever a delivery binds a single physician's
calendar for an uncertain interval and a cesarean does not, the convenience premium
exists and should leave the same calendar signature, and the weekday-weekend
gradient measured within a market and a day is a cheap test that any system with
dated birth records can run. Whether that premium is large enough to move practice
elsewhere is an empirical question our data cannot settle for other countries.
