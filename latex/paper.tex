\begin{abstract}
\noindent Brazil's private health-insurance sector delivers about 80\% of its
babies by cesarean section, the highest rate documented for any large health
system. Using the universe of private insurance claims (TISS) and of birth
records (SINASC), I show that this epidemic is not explained by the standard
financial story: once the hourly labor-assistance fee is counted, a cesarean
pays physicians less than a vaginal delivery in the largest states, and cesarean
rates do not respond to fee variation, or even to a court order to triple
vaginal-delivery pay. The evidence instead points to the physician's time.
Cesareans cluster on weekdays and business hours, fall by 8 percentage points on
weekends and 5 on holidays, and the entire dip comes from cesareans scheduled
before labor begins. The pattern survives among low-risk first-time mothers in
spontaneous labor, is larger where obstetricians are scarce, and is not driven
by educated mothers' requests. Scheduling shifts births toward 37--38 weeks of
gestation, and practice style, not case-mix, accounts for 71\% of the 35
percentage-point private-public gap. A simple model of physician time allocation
rationalizes all of these facts and explains why fee-based policies have failed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Cesarean section is the most common major surgery in the world, and its use has
risen far beyond any clinical benchmark. About 21\% of births worldwide are
cesarean deliveries, a share projected to reach nearly 29\% by 2030
\citep{betran2021}, against a World Health Organization reference of 10--15\%,
the range above which population-level gains in maternal and newborn survival
disappear \citep{who2015cesarean}. Overuse is not innocuous: unnecessary
cesareans raise maternal morbidity, respiratory distress and other neonatal
complications, and costs \citep{sandall2018,boerma2018}. Brazil is the frontier
case. Roughly 57\% of all Brazilian births are cesarean, and in the private
insurance sector, which covers about a quarter of the population, the rate
reaches 80\%, the highest documented for any large health system. The excess is
not confined to complicated pregnancies. Among first-time mothers at term with a
single, well-positioned baby who are already in spontaneous labor, the group for
which surgery is hardest to justify on clinical grounds, two thirds of private
weekday deliveries end in a cesarean, against roughly one third in public
hospitals. Understanding what sustains surgical rates of this magnitude in a
modern, well-financed health sector is a first-order question for health policy
well beyond Brazil.

Economic theory offers a natural suspect and a large literature to back it. When
patients cannot judge treatment necessity, physicians act as imperfect agents
and can tilt clinical decisions toward their own interests \citep{mcguire2000}.
The canonical version of this argument is financial: providers do more of what
pays more. \citet{gruber1996physician} show that obstetricians substitute toward
cesareans when fee differentials widen, \citet{gruber1999physician} confirm the
fee margin in Medicaid data, and \citet{clemens2014} document strong treatment
responses to payment changes in Medicare. Childbirth has become the laboratory
of choice for testing physician agency, precisely because the cesarean decision
is frequent, discretionary, and well measured: \citet{currie2008} show that
liability pressure distorts delivery mode, \citet{johnson2016} find that
physicians treat informed patients (other physicians) differently from everyone
else, and \citet{card2023} show that being delivered in a hospital with a high
cesarean propensity harms the marginal newborn. Throughout this literature the
operative margin is money. Yet the physician's scarcest input is not billable
revenue but time. A vaginal delivery is a long commitment of uncertain onset and
duration that blocks nights, weekends, and office hours; a scheduled cesarean is
a one-hour appointment at a time of the physician's choosing. Separating this
time-and-convenience channel from the fee channel requires observing actual
physician fees and the exact timing of births in the same setting, a combination
administrative data rarely provide.

This paper separates the two channels at national scale. I combine the universe
of hospital claims in Brazil's private insurance system (TISS), which records
the physician fee actually billed for every delivery from 2015 to 2025, with the
universe of birth records (SINASC), 42 million births from 2010 to 2024 with the
exact date and hour of birth, the Robson clinical classification, and an
indicator for whether a cesarean was performed before labor began. The claims
data measure the money incentive; the birth records reveal the time incentive
through the calendar. Three sets of facts emerge.

First, the epidemic is not a price story. The correct financial comparison must
credit the vaginal delivery with the separately billed hourly labor-assistance
fee, which averages 2.7 hours at roughly R\$409 per hour. Once it is counted, a
cesarean pays the physician 10 to 30\% less than a vaginal delivery in Brazil's
largest states, which nonetheless run cesarean rates near 80\%. Across
municipalities and years, the association between the fee gap and the cesarean
rate is tiny and flips sign across specifications. State-level fee swings as
large as two log points leave cesarean rates unchanged. Even a 2015 federal
court order directing the national regulator to make insurers pay at least three
times more for vaginal deliveries produced no movement in fees or in cesarean
rates. Money does not explain where, when, or how often Brazilian physicians
operate.

Second, the calendar does. Because the onset of spontaneous labor does not
respect the week, the day on which a birth falls is as good as random with
respect to medical need, and any systematic weekly pattern in surgery reveals
choices about timing. Cesarean rates in the private sector fall by 8.3
percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points on national holidays, roughly twice
the public-sector dips, and half of all cesareans are performed in weekday
business hours, against a 30\% benchmark under uniform timing. The mechanism is
visible in its components: the weekend dip is minus 9.7 percentage points for
cesareans scheduled before labor and plus 1.7 points for cesareans during labor,
so the entire pattern comes from surgery booked around the physician's calendar,
with a small displacement of unscheduled women into in-labor surgery. The
pattern persists among low-risk first-time mothers in spontaneous labor, is 2.2
points larger in municipalities where obstetricians are scarce relative to
births, and is no stronger for educated mothers, which points to the supply
side rather than to maternal demand for convenient timing.

Third, convenience has a quantity and a cost. A decomposition over Robson
clinical groups attributes 71\% of the 35 percentage-point private-public gap to
practice style within identical clinical groups, not to differences in case-mix.
Benchmarking each municipality's weekday cesarean propensity against its own
weekend rate implies about 50,000 excess weekday cesareans per year in the
private sector alone, about one in ten private cesareans. Scheduling also moves
births earlier: private births are 10.9 percentage points more likely to occur
at 37--38 weeks of gestation than public births after controlling for maternal
age, education, and race, and prelabor cesareans mass exactly at those weeks.
Early-term delivery is precisely the margin that the clinical literature links
to higher neonatal respiratory morbidity and intensive-care admission
\citep{tita2009}, and quasi-experimental evidence confirms that non-indicated
cesareans harm newborn health \citep{costaramon2018,card2023}.

A simple model of physician time allocation, presented in
Appendix~\ref{sec:model}, rationalizes all of these facts jointly. A physician
chooses between attending a vaginal delivery, which arrives at a random hour,
lasts long, and blocks the calendar, and scheduling a one-hour cesarean at the
time she prefers. The choice depends on the fee gap plus a convenience premium:
the value of converting an unschedulable liability into a business-hours
appointment. The premium is positive even where the fee gap is negative, which
is exactly the configuration the data show. The model delivers the fee
irrelevance, the weekday and business-hour clustering of prelabor cesareans, the
larger dips where obstetric time is scarce, and the early-term shift as a single
package, and it explains why fee-based policy levers have no traction: they act
on the wrong wedge.

The policy record is consistent with this reading. Beyond the toothless court
order, I evaluate Parto Adequado, the flagship national quality program run by
the regulator with participating hospitals from 2015 onward. Municipalities
hosting participating private hospitals do show falling cesarean rates, but the
decline starts in 2010, seven years before the program's dissemination phase,
and shows no break at adoption; an event study with a long pre-period rejects
parallel trends, and controlling for the pre-existing trend eliminates the
estimated effect entirely. The decline is real, but its attribution to the
program is not supported. If the binding constraint is the physician's time,
the relevant policy class is organizational: on-call and shift coverage,
midwife-led continuity of care, and hospital scheduling norms, rather than fee
schedules.

The paper contributes to three literatures. To the physician-agency and
supplier-induced-demand literature \citep{mcguire2000,gruber1996physician,%
gruber1999physician,clemens2014,johnson2016}, it adds a setting in which the
canonical financial channel can be measured directly and rejected, and a
time-and-convenience channel that is measured directly and confirmed; agency
here operates through the opportunity cost of physician time, not through the
fee schedule. To the economics of childbirth and cesarean choice
\citep{currie2008,johnson2016,costaramon2018,card2023}, it contributes the first
national anatomy of the world's most extreme cesarean system, built from the
universe of private claims and birth records, and a set of scheduling
fingerprints (weekends, holidays, hours, prelabor status) that can be replicated
in any country with dated birth records. To health policy in middle-income
countries, it explains why a decade of fee-oriented and transparency-oriented
interventions failed to move the epidemic, and points to the reorganization of
obstetric time as the margin worth regulating.
