\begin{abstract}
\noindent Brazil's for-profit hospitals deliver about 81 percent of their births by cesarean, yet under private insurance a cesarean pays the physician less, on average, than a vaginal delivery once the hourly labor-assistance fee is counted. We combine private-insurance claims recording physician fees with 42 million live-birth records identifying cesareans performed before labor. In the five largest states a cesarean pays 18 to 38 percent less per delivery, and the literature's fee responses imply at most 1.5 percentage points from a gap of this size. Per hour of physician time the ranking can reverse: a one-hour cesarean pays more, on average, than a vaginal delivery lasting over about 1.25 hours. For-profit cesarean rates fall 7.9 percentage points on weekends and public ones 6.7, mostly among cesareans performed before labor. In the same municipality and day, the for-profit decline is 1.8 points larger on weekends and 2.5 on holidays, essentially unchanged after controlling for maternal composition. Differences in cesarean rates within Robson clinical groups account for 73 percent of the 36-point for-profit–public gap, and for-profit births are 12.2 points more likely to be early-term after maternal controls. Most of the calendar gradient is common to both sectors, which is consistent with common organizational constraints; the for-profit increment suggests an additional cost of physician time only partly covered by the fee schedule. The evidence makes the organization of obstetric time a promising policy margin.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Cesareans account for about 56 percent of births in Brazil, compared with a worldwide share of 21 percent estimated by
\citet{betran2021}. The rate reaches approximately 84 percent among privately insured deliveries and 81 percent at for-profit birth establishments. The frequency of this major procedure makes Brazil an important setting for studying how payments and the organization of care shape medical decisions. These high rates are not confined to mothers with obvious obstetric indications. Among first-time mothers at term carrying a single cephalic fetus who enter spontaneous labor, 68 percent of weekday births in for-profit establishments end in cesarean delivery, against 36 percent in public establishments. These decisions have clinical consequences: prior cesarean delivery is associated with increased risks in subsequent pregnancies, including abnormal placentation and stillbirth \citep{sandall2018}, while elective repeat cesareans before 39 weeks are associated with higher neonatal respiratory morbidity \citep{tita2009}. Explaining this variation is important for understanding the production of maternity care, not for imposing a universal target cesarean rate: the World Health Organization recommends assessing clinical appropriateness rather than treating any single population percentage as optimal \citep{who2015cesarean}.

The standard economic analysis emphasizes physician agency and relative reimbursement. When one procedure pays more than another, a physician who imperfectly represents the patient's interests may favor the better-paid procedure \citep{dranove1988, mcguire2000}. \citet{gruber1996physician} find that falling fertility and the associated income pressure increased cesarean use; \citet{gruber1999physician} estimate responses to differences in fees across Medicaid programs. \citet{grant2009} obtains a smaller fee response after correcting the earlier data. In Chile, \citet{elejalde2021} find an 8.6-percentage-point increase in cesareans after publicly insured women moved into privately managed hospitals paid equally for the two delivery modes, suggesting schedulability as an incentive distinct from procedure payments. Yet a delivery has another cost that a fee schedule need not capture: the time a physician must reserve \citep{brown1996}. Vaginal delivery has an uncertain onset and duration, often requiring availability outside regular working hours. A planned cesarean can be assigned a relatively short and predictable slot. Hence the price of a procedure and the opportunity cost of the physician's time need not favor the same delivery mode.

This paper examines two related incentives behind high cesarean use: payment for each mode of delivery and the physician time required to provide it. These margins need not move together. A positive cesarean–vaginal fee differential could reinforce surgical delivery. A negative average differential, combined with the concentration of prelabor cesareans during ordinary working hours, suggests that procedure payments do not measure all the relevant incentives. A calendar gradient shared with public hospitals, where shift-based coverage is more common, would also be difficult to attribute entirely to an individually chosen obstetrician's preferences.

We use two complementary administrative sources. The first consists of private-insurance hospital claims submitted under the national \textit{Troca de Informações na Saúde Suplementar} (TISS) standard from 2015 to 2024. Unlike most studies using birth registers, these claims allow us to measure the delivery fee and the separately billed hourly fee for labor assistance. The second is the national live-birth register (Sistema de Informações sobre Nascidos Vivos, SINASC), covering approximately 42 million births during 2010–2024. It provides the day and hour of delivery, measures of obstetric risk, and an indicator for whether a cesarean was performed before labor began. These sources are not linked at the individual level: the first identifies insurance-financed deliveries, while the second identifies births by the legal ownership of the delivery establishment. We use each source for the dimension it measures and do not treat the two populations as interchangeable.

The claims do not support a simple account based on a higher physician fee per cesarean delivery. Vaginal delivery can generate both a base fee and additional hourly labor-assistance charges; among vaginal deliveries with a recorded delivery fee, 40.5 percent also bill labor assistance, averaging 2.9 billed hours when present. Once those charges are included, the mean physician fee for a cesarean is 18 to 38 percent lower than the mean for a vaginal delivery in the five largest states, where private-insurance cesarean rates range from 78 percent to 90 percent. The ranking can reverse per hour. At the observed mean fees, a one-hour cesarean pays more per hour than a vaginal delivery occupying the physician for approximately 1.25 hours or longer. This threshold is an illustrative calculation rather than a direct measurement of time use: the claims record billed labor hours, not the full duration of attendance or on-call availability. A calibration using the fee responses in \citet{gruber1999physician} and \citet{grant2009} suggests that the measured negative fee gaps would imply changes on the order of at most 1.5 percentage points in cesarean use, far smaller than Brazil's sector gap. Our own observational fee regressions are not precise or stable enough to establish a negligible causal price response. We therefore treat the calibration as a comparison of magnitudes, not a causal estimate for Brazil.

The birth records reveal a sharp weekly pattern. For-profit cesarean rates decline by 7.9 percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points on holidays; the corresponding declines in public establishments are 6.7 and 3.5 points. Most of the calendar pattern is therefore shared across ownership types. To separate that common component from the additional for-profit gradient, we compare births in the two sectors within the same municipality and date. This specification absorbs any local daily shock common to both sectors. The additional for-profit decline is 1.8 percentage points on weekends and 2.5 points on holidays, and changes little when we control for observed maternal composition. The contrast is concentrated in cesareans performed before labor begins; cesareans performed during labor move in the opposite direction in the ownership differential. When the specification is re-estimated within establishment-years, half or more of the weekend differential survives, depending on how establishments are weighted. The balance reflects changes in where weekend births occur, as well as possible differences in the patients delivered. These coefficients describe calendar sorting and do not identify the causal effect of weekend assignment or the number of unnecessary cesareans.

Clinical and organizational evidence sharpens, but also limits, the interpretation. The differential is particularly evident among term, singleton, cephalic pregnancies without a previous cesarean, where management is more discretionary. Larger obstetric services have flatter calendar gradients, although bed capacity and delivery volume cannot be separately identified with precision in the most demanding specification. Tests designed to distinguish a physician-leisure account from alternative organizational mechanisms do not support the sharper physician-leisure predictions: ordinary fixed-date holidays that create longer breaks do not generate larger for-profit dips than isolated holidays, and scheduled procedures do not bunch immediately before those breaks. Nor do establishments with more prelabor cesareans display the smoother week-to-week caseload predicted by the hospital demand-smoothing mechanism in \citet{elejalde2021}. The data are consistent with scheduling around the working week, but do not isolate the physician's personal calendar from staffing, operating-room allocation, and hospital protocols.

Differences in cesarean rates across clinical groups are substantial. A Kitagawa decomposition over the ten Robson groups attributes 73 percent of the 36-percentage-point for-profit–public cesarean gap to differences in cesarean rates within clinical groups, with 27 percent attributable to group composition. This is a descriptive accounting exercise: within-group differences also contain unobserved clinical risk and patient preferences, and should not be interpreted as a causal measure of physician behavior. For-profit births are also 12.2 percentage points more likely to occur at 37–38 gestational weeks after adjustment for maternal characteristics. Earlier delivery is a plausible consequence of booking surgery before spontaneous labor, and clinical evidence associates elective early-term birth with worse neonatal outcomes \citep{tita2009}. We cannot attribute that sector difference, or any neonatal outcome, causally to scheduling.

Our main contribution is to distinguish billed procedure fees from the cost of organizing obstetric time. Brazilian calendar studies by \citet{melo2024} and \citet{spinola2026} use birth records to study displacement around Carnival and other inconvenient dates; those records do not contain physician payments. The pattern is not specific to Brazil: in California, births fall by about 18 percent on and just after holidays, cesareans account for about half of the decline, and the missing births move to nearby days \citep{jacobson2021}, while cesarean timing over the day is flatter for patients of group-model health maintenance organizations (HMOs) \citep{spetz2001}. We place billing and birth timing alongside each other in the same national setting, while respecting their different populations. The Chilean evidence of \citet{elejalde2021} is the closest comparison for hospital schedulability. Its demand-smoothing mechanism concerns delivery volume across weeks; our strongest findings concern sorting within the week, and our separate weekly-demand tests do not confirm patient-flow leveling.

The results also relate to variations in physician and hospital practice. Studies of health-care markets emphasize provider-specific treatment patterns and the importance of the practice environment \citep{epstein2009,finkelstein2016,molitor2018,cutler2019}. \citet{fischer2026} provide cesarean-specific evidence that delivery location strongly affects surgical use. Research on staffing, hospital crowding, and obstetric capacity offers plausible organizational channels \citep{maibom2021,facchini2022,bachner2024,bensnes2026}. \citet{parfitt2026} study temperature shocks and maternity-ward staffing in Brazil, with a different estimand; our municipality-by-date effects absorb local daily shocks shared by the two ownership sectors. None of these comparisons isolates the private opportunity cost of one physician from hospital staffing and protocols in our data.

Brazilian policies provide institutional context but not causal identification for this analysis. \citet{melo2023} estimate that a national policy reduced cesarean use by about 1.6 percentage points, while the for-profit rate was still about 78 percent in 2024. A judicial order to increase vaginal delivery payments was never implemented as a fee shock. The voluntary \textit{Parto Adequado} program has pre-existing outcome trends that prevent a credible causal evaluation from our available participation information. The policy evidence motivates prospective tests of coverage and scheduling arrangements instead of identifying their effects here.

These findings do not imply that fees are irrelevant or that scheduled cesareans are necessarily inappropriate. The observed average per-procedure fee differential does not explain the Brazilian pattern on its own. The calendar evidence motivates testing organizational changes, but does not evaluate any such intervention. The remainder of the paper describes the institutional setting and data, states the empirical designs and their limits, examines the payment and scheduling margins, and then relates the patterns to gestational age and clinical composition.

\section{Institutional setting}\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================
% =============================================================================
\subsection{Insurance, hospital ownership, and delivery care}
% =============================================================================
Brazil operates a universal public health system (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS) alongside a regulated private-insurance market covering approximately one quarter of the population. Private insurers contract with physicians and hospitals, generally paying obstetricians fees for delivery-related procedures. Public maternity care more commonly assigns patients to duty teams, while privately financed childbirth often involves continuity with the obstetrician who provided prenatal care. In a national 2011–2012 hospital cohort, approximately 77 percent of privately paid deliveries were attended by the prenatal-care professional, compared with 9 percent of publicly paid deliveries \citep{domingues2014}. This distinction makes control over delivery time potentially salient to the individual physician; it does not imply that public establishments are unaffected by scheduling constraints.

The administrative sources classify sectors differently. TISS records private-insurance-financed events. SINASC records the legal ownership of the establishment in which the birth occurred, which we match to the national registry of health establishments (Cadastro Nacional de Estabelecimentos de Saúde, CNES). A for-profit hospital may serve SUS patients, and an insured mother may use a nonprofit hospital. In CNES, 99 percent of obstetric beds in public establishments are designated for SUS use, compared with 22 percent in for-profit and 72 percent in nonprofit establishments. These figures justify a substantive for-profit–public comparison but not an equivalence between provider ownership and payer. We report nonprofit establishments descriptively and exclude them from the principal two-sector contrast.

The cesarean share in private-insurance claims remains roughly 82 to 86 percent across 2015–2024 (Figure~\ref{fig:trend}). The share in for-profit establishments is about 81 percent over the longer period, while the public-establishment share rises from approximately 37 percent in 2010 to 52 percent in 2024. The rates and trajectories are descriptive; the two private-sector series refer to different populations.

\subsection{What the fee schedule pays}
% =============================================================================

Private-insurance billing distinguishes the fee for the delivery itself from a separate hourly fee for assistance during labor. The appropriate per-delivery fee comparison therefore credits vaginal delivery with both components. Whether the labor-assistance service is billed varies substantially across states and contracts; its absence is not proof that the physician spent no time attending labor.

A second payment, incompletely observed in the data, may compensate an obstetrician for being personally available whenever labor begins. Brazilian professional guidance and private-insurance regulation place restrictions on this availability fee \citep{cfm2012disponibilidade, ans2014disponibilidade}. The claims do not measure payments made privately under such arrangements. Deliveries lacking a physician-delivery charge to the insurer correspond to 4.5 percent of private-insurance deliveries. This fraction indicates the limit of the claims-based fee measure; it is not an estimate of the prevalence or value of privately paid availability arrangements. Other unobserved compensation, including bonuses, ownership returns, and long-run practice incentives, also lies outside the billing measure.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{The policy environment}

A 2015 regulation by the Agência Nacional de Saúde Suplementar (ANS), the private-insurance regulator, strengthened disclosure of provider-specific cesarean rates and documentation requirements for elective procedures. A separate December 2015 judicial order called for plans to pay substantially more for vaginal deliveries, but the order was appealed and did not generate the proposed change in reimbursement; it therefore provides no implemented policy shock. The voluntary \textit{Parto Adequado} quality-improvement initiative expanded after a 2015–2016 pilot. The available participant list does not identify hospital-specific entry dates, and participating hospitals exhibit declining cesarean trends before a common assumed 2017 onset. Consequently, the event-study evidence in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{app:policy} cannot credibly identify the initiative's effect. \citet{melo2023} estimate that the broader national policy reduced cesarean use by approximately 1.6 percentage points. We treat these episodes as context, not as quasi-experiments underlying this paper.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
% =============================================================================

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Physician fees in TISS}\label{sec:fees_data}
% =============================================================================

Private insurers report hospital claims to the ANS under the mandatory TISS standard. We use delivery claims for 2015–2024, identified by the standardized cesarean and vaginal-delivery procedure codes. At the delivery level we identify physician fees and, for vaginal births, charges for hourly labor assistance. The claims also identify the municipality, beneficiary age band, diagnosis codes, and billed hospital amounts. Delivery codes determine mode even when a positive physician fee is not recorded. An insurer-financed delivery does not necessarily contain a positive physician-delivery fee; the observed fee averages condition on billed positive fees as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:data}.

The economic fee for vaginal delivery is the sum of the delivery fee and any hourly labor-assistance fee billed for that birth, with the latter set to zero when it is not billed. At the municipality-year level we compare its average with the average cesarean delivery fee. The resulting log ratio is a market-level measure of billed relative fees; it is not necessarily the fee faced by a particular physician choosing a patient's mode of delivery. For exploratory associations, we add municipal GDP per capita, health-plan coverage, adequacy of prenatal care, and obstetrician density from the Instituto de Estudos para Políticas de Saúde (IEPS) and CNES. The main fee panel has 5,198 municipality-years with at least 20 private-insurance deliveries and an observed fee ratio; specifications with all municipal covariates have 4,706 observations.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Birth timing and clinical characteristics in SINASC}\label{sec:timing_data}
% =============================================================================

SINASC records 42,006,849 live births over 2010–2024 and does not cover stillbirths. It includes the delivery date and hour, mode of delivery, gestational age, maternal age, schooling and race, parity, selected pregnancy characteristics, and, in later years, the WHO Robson clinical classification and whether a cesarean preceded the onset of labor. We link births to the birth establishment and its legal-entity type in CNES. The principal timing outcomes are daily sector-specific shares of births delivered by cesarean, by prelabor cesarean, and by cesarean during labor.

Measurement availability differs over time. The Robson classification is filled from 2014 onwards. The labor timing indicator is usable from 2012 and is absent for approximately 7 percent of for-profit and 10 percent of public cesareans during 2012–2024. Missingness is nearly identical on weekdays and weekends within each sector, although that balance fails for Robson group 1. Parity is entirely unrecorded in the extract distributed by the Departamento de Informação e Informática do Sistema Único de Saúde (DATASUS) for 2010–2013, becoming available in 2014; maternal race is sparsely reported before approximately 2012. Gestational-age reporting is also sparse in 2010 and incomplete in 2011. We show sample periods and denominators for every outcome; the full sample flow appears in \satab{tab:sampleflow}.

The two files illuminate related but distinct mechanisms. We observe fees in private-insurance claims and delivery timing in the national birth register; we do not link a fee and a delivery timestamp for the same patient or physician. This prevents a structural or reduced-form estimate of the effect of an individual physician's schedule on delivery mode. It also makes the ownership comparisons fundamentally descriptive, despite the saturated fixed effects used below.

\section{Empirical approach and interpretation}\label{sec:strategy}
% =============================================================================
Our analysis asks three complementary questions. First, do areas with more favorable billed cesarean fees have higher cesarean rates? Second, how does the distribution of cesarean delivery across the calendar differ between for-profit and public establishments? Third, how much of the sector gap remains after accounting for observable obstetric groups, and how does birth timing correlate with gestational age? These are distinct estimands, not successive estimates of one causal parameter.

\subsection{Relative fees}

For municipality $m$ in year $t$, we estimate
\begin{equation}
C_{mt}=\beta\log\left(\frac{f^c_{mt}}{f^v_{mt}}\right)+X_{mt}'\gamma+\mu_m+\delta_t+\varepsilon_{mt},
\label{eq:feegap}
\end{equation}
where \(C_{mt}\) is the cesarean share in private-insurance claims and \(f^v\) includes hourly labor assistance. We estimate specifications with state or municipality fixed effects, year fixed effects and, in the fully adjusted model, municipal controls. Observations are weighted by private-insurance deliveries. Standard errors are clustered by the corresponding fixed-effect geography. An additional state-year first-difference exercise asks whether large annual changes in the aggregate fee ratio coincide with changes in cesarean rates; because there are only 22 state clusters, inference is complemented by a Webb-weight wild-cluster bootstrap.

Because billed fees vary with insurers, patient mix, contracting, and market structure, the coefficient $\beta$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:feegap} is not a causal price response. The fee averages can also be noisy, especially where claims volume is low. We therefore interpret the coefficient as an association and rely separately on a transparent, necessarily approximate calibration from prior quasi-experimental estimates. Failure to reject zero is not evidence of equivalence; the confidence intervals do not establish a response tightly concentrated near zero.

\subsection{Calendar gradients}

For municipality $m$, delivery date $d$, and ownership sector $s$, let \(Y_{mds}\) denote the share of observed births delivered by cesarean. We first estimate each sector's calendar gradient:
\begin{equation}
Y_{mds}=b_{1s}\,\mathrm{Weekend}_d+b_{2s}\,\mathrm{Holiday}_d+
 b_{3s}\,\mathrm{Eve}_d+\mu_{ms}+\delta_{ts}+e_{mds}.
\label{eq:scheduling}
\end{equation}

The specification uses municipality and year fixed effects separately by sector, weights cells by the number of births, and clusters standard errors two ways, by municipality and date. Holidays and eves are defined in Appendix~\ref{app:data}; the holiday contrasts are conditional on the other calendar indicators. The coefficients describe the composition of births observed on different dates. They are not effects of randomly assigning otherwise identical pregnancies to weekends, because scheduling changes which pregnancies are delivered on those days.

The primary ownership comparison pools both sectors and absorbs all local daily shocks that affect them equally:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Y_{mds}={}&\gamma_1(\mathrm{ForProfit}_s\times\mathrm{Weekend}_d)
+\gamma_2(\mathrm{ForProfit}_s\times\mathrm{Holiday}_d)\\
&+\gamma_3(\mathrm{ForProfit}_s\times\mathrm{Eve}_d)
+\lambda_{md}+\phi_{ms}+u_{mds}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gradient}
\end{equation}
Here \(\lambda_{md}\) are municipality-by-date effects and \(\phi_{ms}\) municipality-by-sector effects. The parameters \(\gamma_j\) measure how much more the for-profit cesarean share changes on rest days than the public share in the same local market. Identification comes from municipality-days with births recorded in both sectors: 461 municipalities accounting for 74 percent of for-profit and 56 percent of public births (\satab{tab:ident_sample}). A causal interpretation would require that the sectors would have shared the same within-municipality weekend and holiday gradients absent the organization of care specific to for-profit providers. Ownership, patient sorting, and the timing of actual deliveries are not randomly assigned, so we report the coefficients as within-municipality-day differentials, not as causal effects.

Controls for the maternal age and education distribution in each municipality, day, and sector, together with race and parity when recorded, probe observable differences in composition. Column 2 of Table~\ref{tab:main_gradient} retains the 2010–2024 sample, but the parity field is blank before 2014 and race is largely available only from approximately 2012; these characteristics therefore do not provide full-period composition adjustment. The point estimate of the weekend interaction is virtually unchanged relative to column 1. The first-birth outcome in the predetermined-characteristics balance table (\satab{tab:balance}) is also estimated only where parity is recorded, beginning in 2014. Adding Robson-group shares provides a stricter clinical-composition adjustment, but the Robson groups partly encode labor onset and gestational age, which delivery management may change. That specification is descriptive and is not our preferred control set. We complement the share regressions with count outcomes, within-establishment comparisons, subgroup profiles, and holiday event studies. Counts help distinguish changes in delivery intensity from changes in the numerator of a share; they still do not reveal which deliveries would have occurred by a different mode absent scheduling.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Mechanisms and limits}
% =============================================================================

A simple model (Appendix~\ref{sec:model}) decomposes the physician's private return from surgery into a relative fee \(f^c-f^v\) and a convenience premium \(\Pi\) from replacing an uncertain labor episode with a scheduled block. The model implies that surgery can be privately attractive even if the billed fee differential is negative, and predicts concentration of prelabor cesareans during low-opportunity-cost hours. However, it does not imply a small marginal response to fees merely because the convenience premium is positive. That response also depends on the distribution of patients near the delivery mode threshold. Nor does a weekly calendar pattern uniquely reveal a doctor's preference for leisure. Hospital schedules, availability of teams, operating-room capacity, and patients' own preferences can create similar gradients. The empirical analysis is designed to show where those interpretations receive support and where they do not.

A lower weekend cesarean rate cannot be converted into a count of procedures caused by scheduling. A delivery may be moved from Sunday to Friday without any change in mode. Such displacement violates the no-interference condition that a simple weekend treatment-effect interpretation would require. The weekday-excess calculation below is a descriptive benchmark, not an estimate of avoidable surgery.

The municipality-year fee association and the municipality-day ownership differential appear side by side in Figure~\ref{fig:two_margins}. The two panels summarize different estimands and do not jointly identify a causal channel.

\section{Fees and the cesarean decision}\label{sec:notprice}
% =============================================================================

% =============================================================================
\subsection{The per-delivery comparison}\label{sec:per_delivery}
% =============================================================================

The average cesarean delivery fee is approximately R\$1,939, while the average base fee for a vaginal delivery is R\$1,893 (\satab{tab:fee_per_hour}). Comparing those two payments alone would produce a small national advantage for the cesarean. But it would omit a substantial component of vaginal-delivery compensation. Among vaginal deliveries with a recorded delivery fee (94.2 percent of all vaginal deliveries), 40.5 percent also bill a separate labor-assistance fee, averaging 2.9 hours at approximately R\$420 per billed hour. The other 59.5 percent have no labor-assistance charge. The expected fee for vaginal delivery is calculated over the same delivery-fee sample, assigning zero additional billed labor-assistance revenue when none is recorded. The billing share and conditional fee means therefore have a common denominator.

With this definition, the relative fee differs materially across states. In Brazil's five largest states by insured deliveries, the mean cesarean fee is 18 to 38 percent lower than the mean economic vaginal fee, despite private-insurance cesarean rates between 78 percent and 90 percent. The finding does not require a regression analysis: the more common procedure is, on average, the less remunerative one per delivery in the largest markets. Billing practices vary, however. Within the same delivery-fee sample, the fraction of vaginal deliveries with a labor-assistance charge ranges from approximately 20 percent in São Paulo to 71 percent in Minas Gerais (20.1 percent and 71.3 percent, respectively). In states where assistance is rarely billed, the vaginal base fee itself can exceed the cesarean fee; in others, the extra hourly component accounts for most of the reversal. Thus the economic fee measure describes expected billed revenue across heterogeneous contracts, not a common national price available to every doctor.

The distinction is also relevant within markets. Within this sample, a vaginal delivery with billed labor assistance pays about R\$3,355 on average; one without it pays about R\$1,778. Weighting these conditional means by the corresponding shares (40.5 percent and 59.5 percent) yields the reported expected vaginal fee of approximately R\$2,417. A physician who expects to bill no additional labor hours may face a positive cesarean fee differential even when the mean vaginal fee in the municipality is higher. Those differences constrain the interpretation of aggregate fee regressions and motivate viewing the comparison as an accounting fact instead of a fully observed private incentive.
% =============================================================================
\subsection{The time-price comparison}\label{sec:time_price}
% =============================================================================

The per-delivery and per-hour comparisons answer different economic questions. For illustration, assume a cesarean occupies one hour of physician time. At an average cesarean fee close to R\$1,940, the physician receives more per hour than from a vaginal delivery whenever the latter occupies more than roughly 1.25 hours at its mean economic fee. If the cesarean instead occupies two hours, the corresponding threshold is approximately 2.5 hours. For vaginal deliveries that bill labor assistance the one-hour cesarean threshold is higher (approximately 1.7 hours); for those that bill none it is lower (approximately 0.9 hours). \satab{tab:fee_per_hour} gives the corresponding state-specific calculations.

The break-even times are examples, not observed physician work hours. We do not see time spent on call, travel, interruptions, hours that are not billed, or the timing of the relevant physician's other work. Billed labor hours are only a partial proxy and cannot establish that an individual doctor earns more per hour from surgery. They nonetheless show why comparing procedure-level fees alone can miss an important incentive. A planned cesarean typically requires a more predictable slot, while vaginal delivery exposes the attending physician and hospital to uncertainty about the time and resources required. The hourly labor-assistance fee is therefore a potentially relevant policy lever on the price of time, although its behavioral effect is not identified here.

These are nominal fee means over 2015–2024. At the average official exchange rate of R\$4.37 per US dollar during those years, R\$1,939 is approximately US\$444 and R\$420 approximately US\$96. At the average purchasing-power-parity conversion factor of R\$2.28 per international dollar, the cesarean fee is approximately 850 international dollars (World Bank, series PA.NUS.FCRF and PA.NUS.PPP). The log fee ratio compares payments within a municipality-year and does not depend on these currency conversions. The fee-response calibration separately uses a purchasing-power adjustment to translate historical US estimates; it is not an estimate of actual Brazilian payment responses.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{How much can the relative fee explain?}
% =============================================================================

Published estimates provide a benchmark for the potential importance of the observed fee differential. \citet{gruber1999physician} report an increase of about four percentage points in the cesarean share for a US\$1,000 increase in the fee gap between cesarean and vaginal deliveries. Re-estimation on corrected data by \citet{grant2009} yields about one point per US\$1,000. Using the purchasing-power adjustment and observed fee levels in \satab{tab:feegap_ci}, these responses correspond to approximately 0.75 and 3.01 percentage points per log point of the fee ratio. Applying them to negative log fee gaps of 0.20–0.49 in Brazil's five largest states implies changes of at most about one third of a percentage point at Grant's magnitude and 1.5 points at the larger \citet{gruber1999physician} magnitude. These are small relative to a 36-point for-profit–public difference. The exercise is only a rough benchmark, however, as it extrapolates estimates from a different country, time period, and payment system; it is not an estimate of Brazil's counterfactual cesarean rate under a fee reform.

Our own fee associations do not provide a clean alternative calibration. Panel A of Table~\ref{tab:fees} shows that the coefficient on the log economic fee ratio changes sign when state fixed effects are replaced by municipality fixed effects. With municipal controls, it is 0.019 under state fixed effects and $-0.013$ under municipality fixed effects, neither statistically different from zero. The state fixed-effect model without controls yields a positive coefficient of 0.049, while the corresponding municipality fixed-effect estimate is $-0.014$. The within-municipality confidence intervals lie below both literature-calibrated responses; the state-level intervals, based on 27 state clusters, are wider and encompass those benchmarks. The unadjusted state-level coefficient, at 4.9 percentage points per log point, is itself above the larger literature response. None of the four confidence intervals lies entirely within a ±2-percentage-point-per-log-point equivalence region (\satab{tab:feegap_ci}). The evidence therefore does not establish that the fee response is negligibly small.

Even large year-to-year movements in the state-level billed fee ratio do not exhibit a stable positive association with cesarean use (Table~\ref{tab:fees}, Panel B). The first-difference estimate is $-0.0087$ in share units and has a wild-cluster-bootstrap $p$-value of 0.323. The state-year levels estimate is likewise imprecise (bootstrap $p = 0.166$). The large annual swings in a fee measure constructed from changing claims composition and contracts make measurement error a serious concern, potentially attenuating within-geography coefficients. For this reason, neither the absence of a stable association nor the negative point estimates should be presented as evidence that physicians do not respond to prices.

The observed mean fee schedule does not show a per-delivery revenue advantage for cesareans in the largest private-insurance markets. Responses of the magnitudes documented in the prior fee literature are insufficient, by themselves, to account for the sector gap. The data cannot rule out stronger responses to an exogenous change in Brazilian fees, or responses to fees and availability payments that are not visible in TISS. The unimplemented 2015 judicial order provides no such experiment.

\section{The calendar organization of cesarean delivery}\label{sec:calendar_organization}
% =============================================================================

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Weekly gradients and the for-profit differential}
% =============================================================================
Cesarean delivery in Brazil is concentrated around the working week. For-profit cesarean rates are high and nearly flat from Monday to Friday, then decline markedly on Saturday and Sunday (Figure~\ref{fig:calendar}(a)). Public establishments have much lower rates but a similar weekly profile. Cesarean births are also concentrated during regular working hours, particularly in the morning; vaginal births are distributed more evenly through the day (Figure~\ref{fig:calendar}(b)). Approximately 50 percent of cesareans in both sectors take place during weekday business hours, versus 29.8 percent under a benchmark in which deliveries occur uniformly across hours of the week. The shared pattern suggests that the allocation of obstetric work is not simply a distinctive feature of privately financed, personally attended births.

With municipality and year fixed effects, the cesarean rate in for-profit establishments falls 7.9 percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points on holidays (Table~\ref{tab:main_gradient}, Panel B). Public establishments experience declines of 6.7 and 3.5 points. The ownership-specific result is the additional for-profit gradient after accounting for the pattern common to public establishments.

Within the same municipality and day, the for-profit cesarean share declines a further 1.79 percentage points on weekends and 2.55 points on holidays, with standard errors of 0.62 and 0.51 (Table~\ref{tab:main_gradient}, Panel A). These estimates come from Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient} with municipality-by-date fixed effects, which absorb a municipality's weather, holiday calendar, local demand conditions, and any other shock shared by the two sectors on the same day. Adding the shares of births in maternal age, schooling, race, and parity categories leaves the estimates at $-1.82$ and $-2.41$ points. Controlling for Robson-group shares, which is more restrictive but partly depends on delivery decisions themselves, reduces them to $-1.49$ and $-1.71$ points. This stability weakens a straightforward explanation based on observed maternal composition, but cannot remove unmeasured risk or sorting across ownership sectors.

The within-municipality-day comparison is identified only from the 461 municipalities where both sectors record births on the same date, and therefore places greatest weight on relatively substantial obstetric markets. In that common support the own-sector weekend effects are $-7.05$ points for for-profit and $-5.27$ for public establishments, leaving essentially the same 1.8-point difference (\satab{tab:ident_sample}). The check is needed because the pooled sector gradients and the ownership interaction are not necessarily estimated on identical sets of births.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Prelabor cesareans and the discretionary margin}\label{sec:prelabor}
% =============================================================================

The timing indicator provides a closer connection between the calendar pattern and a mechanism. A cesarean that precedes labor can be scheduled directly. An intrapartum cesarean is still influenced by clinical and organizational decisions, but its occurrence and timing cannot be managed in the same way once spontaneous labor has begun. The distinction is informative despite incomplete coding and possible displacement between categories.

In the for-profit sector, the share of all births delivered by prelabor cesarean falls 8.93 percentage points on weekends, while the in-labor cesarean share rises 1.86 points (Table~\ref{tab:prelabor_lowrisk}). In the public sector, the corresponding changes are $-4.32$ and $-1.43$ points. The for-profit-versus-public excess weekend decline in Table~\ref{tab:main_gradient} is concentrated in the prelabor component ($-4.75$ points), offset by a positive difference in the intrapartum component (2.88 points). A decline in the prelabor share does not by itself show that a procedure would otherwise have been performed at a different mode; it may reflect a scheduled cesarean moved to an adjacent weekday. The opposite movement in intrapartum cesareans is consistent with such sorting, although selection in the women who actually deliver on a weekend offers an alternative explanation.

The pattern is not restricted to pregnancies at high baseline cesarean risk. Among Robson groups 1 and 2, which cover first-time mothers at term with a singleton, cephalic fetus, the for-profit weekend decline is 7.03 points. In group 1 alone, defined to include spontaneous labor, it is 6.04 points (Table~\ref{tab:prelabor_lowrisk}, columns 5 and 6). The group 1 gradient limits an explanation based exclusively on booking cesareans before labor. Group 1 contains no cesareans classified as prelabor by construction; approximately 4.7 percentage points of its dip come from cesareans classified as performed during labor. The remaining approximately 1.3 points are deliveries with missing timing information, which are more common on weekdays in that group (11.2 percent against 9.8 percent on weekends) and may include uncoded prelabor cesareans (Supplementary Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:robson_validation} and~\ref{tab:timing_missing}). The in-labor component documents a pattern extending beyond directly scheduled surgery, but cannot distinguish a change in intrapartum treatment thresholds from composition among women arriving in labor.

Across Robson groups, the larger for-profit weekend declines are concentrated in groups 1–4, encompassing term singleton cephalic pregnancies without a previous cesarean, where mode choice is relatively open (Figure~\ref{fig:calendar}(c)). In group 5, with a previous cesarean, the for-profit weekend drop is smaller than in the public sector; the for-profit weekday cesarean rate already approaches 95 percent, leaving little scope for further mode switching. The gradient is likewise compressed among breech, multiple, and abnormal-lie pregnancies for which baseline cesarean rates approach the ceiling. Among preterm births (group 10), the two sectors' gradients are similar. The excess for-profit gradient is thus concentrated where the choice between labor and surgery is still open, but some Robson categories depend on the same labor-onset and gestational-age choices being studied. These comparisons should not be used as clean untreated placebo groups. A separate municipality-by-date subgroup analysis compares the ownership differential among term and preterm births: the for-profit weekend differential is $-2.11$ percentage points at term and $+1.47$ points before 37 weeks (\satab{tab:subgroup_gradients}). These are not the group-10 own-sector gradients shown in Figure~\ref{fig:calendar}(c), and gestational age is partly determined by delivery management.

\subsection{Within-establishment behavior and hospital activity}\label{sec:within_estab}
% =============================================================================

A municipality-sector gradient combines changes in practice at a given establishment with changes in which establishments deliver births on weekends. \satab{tab:estab_gradient} separates those components. Re-estimating the ownership interaction within establishment-years, while retaining municipality-by-date effects, yields a weekend differential of $-0.95$ points when weighted by daily births and $-1.26$ points when establishment weights are held at average daily births. The headline municipality-sector effect on the same eligible births is $-1.79$ points. The point estimates indicate that half or more of the weekend differential survives within establishments, depending on the weighting scheme (0.95/1.79 under birth weights; 1.26/1.79 using fixed establishment weights as an illustrative, not strictly like-for-like ratio). Changes in the distribution of births across hospitals account for the remainder, though the ratios are not a formal causal decomposition.

A balanced establishment-day panel shows that for-profit establishments are 8.7 percentage points less likely than public establishments to record any weekend birth, conditional on establishment-year and municipality-by-date effects (\satab{tab:estab_gradient}). The counts of prelabor cesareans decline much more sharply than vaginal births. This is evidence of ownership-specific organization of deliveries, but it does not allow a decomposition into individual doctors' decisions, staffing policies, or patients' choice of establishment. If hospitals differ in whether and how they operate maternity services on weekends, that itself can generate part of the ownership differential.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Holidays, capacity, and competing mechanisms}\label{sec:capacity}
% =============================================================================

The simplest individual-convenience narrative makes predictions beyond a weekend dip. If doctors move procedures to preserve longer uninterrupted breaks, a holiday creating a three- or four-day weekend should discourage for-profit cesareans more than an isolated Wednesday holiday, and prelabor procedures should accumulate in the days immediately beforehand. These are useful opportunities to challenge the mechanism rather than confirm it by construction.

They do not provide the predicted support. Among ordinary fixed-date holidays, the for-profit differential in prelabor cesarean shares is similarly negative for bridge, three-day, and isolated holidays. Tests of equality between the corresponding holiday-type coefficients have $p$-values above 0.7 for the prelabor outcome (Table~\ref{tab:long_weekends}). Event-time regressions for counts likewise show no pre-holiday bunching within the three-day window examined: the summed for-profit-minus-public prelabor difference during the three days before a bridge holiday is $-0.47$ deliveries per municipality, instead of positive. Over a seven-day window, for-profit relative activity declines in prelabor cesareans, in-labor cesareans, vaginal births, and total births. These estimates indicate lower relative for-profit delivery activity throughout the observed holiday window. They do not establish that deliveries were not rescheduled outside that window, or that any particular hospital closed. The estimates do not refute birth displacement around exceptional holidays such as Carnival, studied by \citet{melo2024}; those holidays are excluded from the fixed-date comparison.

Hospital scale offers a second lens. In linked CNES data, larger for-profit obstetric services tend to have less negative weekend gradients in the prelabor cesarean share. When lagged log annual delivery volume and lagged log obstetric beds enter together with date effects, the volume interaction is about $1.49$ percentage points per log point (SE 0.34), while the bed interaction is smaller and not significant. With the stronger municipality-by-date effects, the corresponding estimates shrink to $0.64$ (SE 0.41) and $0.58$ (SE 0.38): jointly significant ($p \approx 0.02$) but neither individually distinguishable from zero. Alternative one-at-a-time specifications support an association with service scale, not a clean separation between beds, delivery volume, and availability of physicians. These variables are predetermined by one year but not randomly assigned. Their coefficients should not be interpreted as the causal effect of capacity.

A further alternative mechanism is demand smoothing across weeks. \citet{elejalde2021} argue that schedulable deliveries let hospitals move activity away from weeks with anticipated high demand. We examine whether an establishment's prelabor cesarean share rises with an out-of-sample forecast of the following week's demand, and whether establishments with more prelabor cesareans experience a smoother weekly delivery flow (\satab{tab:demand_smoothing}). Neither prediction is supported. The first test is weak because the seasonal leave-one-out forecast has little predictive power; the second, better-powered comparison shows no reduction in the variance-to-mean ratio of weekly births. This does not invalidate the Chilean causal result. It indicates that the particular cross-week demand-smoothing pattern is not visible in these Brazilian data.

The concentration of cesareans on working days and the additional for-profit gradient remain consistent with scheduling. The data cannot isolate that increment as an individual obstetrician's preference for weekends off. Organization of maternity services is the more defensible mechanism-level description. Evidence in which work pressure and staffing alter obstetric decisions \citep{maibom2021,facchini2022,bachner2024,bensnes2026} makes institutional scheduling a plausible part of the account.

\section{Gestational timing, clinical composition, and practice variation}\label{sec:cost}
% =============================================================================

% =============================================================================
\subsection{Births at 37--38 weeks and neonatal outcomes}
% =============================================================================

The timing of delivery has consequences beyond the choice of surgical versus vaginal birth. When a cesarean is scheduled before spontaneous labor, booking it earlier can reduce the risk that labor begins before the procedure. For-profit establishments have a substantially earlier gestational-age distribution: approximately 41 percent of births occur at 37--38 completed weeks, compared with 26 percent in public establishments, while 17 percent versus 32 percent occur at 40--41 weeks (\safig{fig:gestation}). Both sectors have their modal week of birth at week 39; the for-profit distribution is shifted earlier, not centered on weeks 37--38. Within for-profit establishments, prelabor cesareans occur earlier than in-labor cesareans, which in turn occur earlier than vaginal births.

With municipality and year fixed effects and maternal age, schooling, and race controls, births in for-profit establishments are 12.18 percentage points more likely to occur at 37--38 weeks than public-establishment births (SE 0.77; Table~\ref{tab:health}). A corresponding municipality-by-date specification shows that the for-profit early-term share falls 1.35 points more than the public share on weekends (\satab{tab:estab_gradient}, Panel B). The estimates indicate that early-term births in the for-profit sector are more concentrated on weekdays. But sector and delivery mode are selected, and the controls do not observe all pregnancy risk, assisted reproduction, reasons for induction, or measurement error in gestational age. The evidence does not identify a causal increase in early-term birth due to provider scheduling.

Clinical evidence nevertheless makes the distinction important. \citet{tita2009} find that elective repeat cesarean delivery at 37--38 rather than 39 weeks is associated with higher adverse neonatal risk, including respiratory complications and intensive-care admission. \citet{costaramon2018} and \citet{borra2019} provide additional evidence on harms associated with non-indicated or early surgical delivery. \citet{card2023}, by contrast, find improved condition at birth and fewer readmissions for some low-risk first births at hospitals with a higher cesarean propensity, alongside more emergency visits for respiratory problems in the year after birth. Cesarean delivery therefore has heterogeneous clinical effects, not a uniformly negative marginal effect. These studies inform the potential health implications of the Brazilian gestational-age difference; they do not convert our estimated sector association into an estimate of harmed children.

In fact, average low-birthweight and low-Apgar rates are lower in for-profit establishments (Table~\ref{tab:health}). This is compatible with favorable maternal socioeconomic composition and access to care, and is one reason unadjusted sector differences in newborn health cannot be interpreted causally. Restricting to term, singleton, cephalic first births narrows the low-birthweight contrast substantially. An ecological check of early-term shares against infant hospital use does not yield statistically distinguishable associations (\satab{tab:neonatal_suggestive}). The substantive outcome documented by this paper is earlier gestational timing in the for-profit sector, not a measured causal effect on neonatal morbidity.

\subsection{Clinical composition versus within-group rates}
% =============================================================================

We assess the role of observed clinical composition by sorting deliveries into ten mutually exclusive Robson groups, based on parity, previous cesarean delivery, gestational age, fetal presentation, number of fetuses, and labor onset. We use the \citet{kitagawa1955} identity to express the sector difference in cesarean rates as the sum of a term reflecting differences in group shares and a term reflecting differences in cesarean rates within groups.

The for-profit--public cesarean gap is 36.2 percentage points over 2014--2024 (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). Differences in Robson-group composition account for 10.0 points (27 percent) and differences in group-specific rates for 26.3 points (73 percent). For-profit rates exceed public rates by large amounts in the most discretionary term singleton cephalic groups. For example, group 1 cesarean rates are 65.8 percent versus 35.1 percent, and group 2 rates are 89.0 percent versus 55.5 percent. In group 5, women with a previous cesarean, they are 94.6 percent versus 78.4 percent. The sector difference is therefore not explained primarily by the distribution across these ten observable obstetric groups.

The second component is a within-Robson-group rate difference. Attributing all of it to physician practice style would require stronger assumptions. Group members can differ in unmeasured obstetric conditions, patient preferences, access to technology, and referral pathways, all of which may vary by sector. Similarly, the Robson variables are not all predetermined relative to scheduling; gestational age and onset of labor may themselves be altered by delivery management. The decomposition demonstrates the limits of observable case-mix accounting, not the causal contribution of physicians to excessive cesareans.

Additional descriptive variation remains within the for-profit sector. Robson-standardized cesarean rates differ substantially across establishments. The standard deviation of the establishment-year standardized rate is approximately 14 percentage points; conditioning on municipality-by-year, maternal composition, and obstetric capacity leaves a residual standard deviation of 6.3 points (\satab{tab:estab_practice_style}). Two for-profit establishments operating in the same municipality and year differ by nearly 12 points on average in the standardized cesarean rate. Such dispersion is consistent with the importance of care environments, but combines unmeasured risk and preferences with clinical management.

% =============================================================================
\subsection{A mechanical weekday-cesarean benchmark}
% =============================================================================

A mechanical benchmark helps express the scale of the within-sector calendar gradient. For each municipality-year, apply its own observed weekend cesarean rate to its weekday birth count and compare the result with actual weekday cesareans. The municipality-specific calculations place about 35,000 for-profit weekday cesareans per year above that reference, around one in eleven weekday cesareans in the sector. This is a transparent accounting exercise. The weekend cesarean rate is not an estimate of the clinically optimal rate, and births delivered by surgery on weekdays may simply have been scheduled away from a weekend. Accordingly the estimate should not be called the number of unnecessary or avoidable procedures.

The mechanical benchmark is distinct from the additional for-profit calendar differential. Applying the roughly 1.8-point within-municipality-day differential to the same approximate number of for-profit weekday births produces a scale figure near 8,000, whereas the gross 7.9-point for-profit weekend gradient corresponds to roughly 37,000 on that base. Neither figure is causal; they answer different descriptive questions, and the more tightly controlled differential nets out the calendar pattern common to public establishments.

Finally, gross hospital amounts billed are similar across modes. In the TISS claims, the mean total billed amount for a cesarean hospitalization is approximately R\$6,992 versus R\$7,093 for a vaginal delivery, with a modestly higher median for cesareans (\satab{tab:cost}). These amounts sum all billing lines and are charges rather than insurer payments or hospital profits. They neither establish the absence of financial incentives nor support a general claim that cesareans are more profitable for hospitals.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

Brazil combines exceptionally high cesarean use with a distinctive arrangement for financing and organizing maternity care. Private-insurance claims reveal that cesareans do not generally generate a higher mean physician fee per delivery in the country's largest insurance markets once separately billed labor assistance is counted. At plausible durations, however, cesareans may provide greater revenue per hour of physician time, and they allow substantially more predictable scheduling. The distinction points to an incentive that is omitted by comparisons of procedure fees alone.

The national birth register shows that cesareans concentrate during ordinary working hours and are less frequent on weekends and holidays. Most of this pattern is common to for-profit and public establishments, but a further for-profit decline of approximately 1.8 percentage points on weekends and 2.5 points on holidays remains within the same municipality and date, with little change after adjustment for observed maternal composition. Much of the difference involves cesareans performed before labor. The evidence is therefore consistent with obstetric scheduling as a feature of how care is supplied.

The limits are central to the interpretation. Relative-fee regressions are observational, unstable across fixed-effect structures, and insufficiently precise to establish a small causal price response. The timing regressions document how births are sorted across dates, not which cesareans would disappear without scheduling. Holiday and demand-smoothing tests fail to confirm several sharper versions of the physician-convenience mechanism. Within-Robson-group rate differences are large, but cannot be entirely equated with physician practice style. The association with earlier gestational age identifies a potentially important health margin, not the effect of changing delivery organization on newborn outcomes.

For policy, the results suggest a research agenda rather than an evaluated intervention. Strengthening laborist or team-based coverage, reducing dependence on one physician's unpredictable on-call time, changing operating-room and staffing protocols, and examining payments for labor assistance are plausible margins for experimentation. Both publicly and privately organized maternity care would need attention, because most of the weekly pattern is shared. Future studies that exploit exogenous reforms to provider coverage or scheduling rules, while observing mode, gestational age, patient risk, and neonatal outcomes, could distinguish whether changes in the organization of time reduce unnecessary intervention without compromising safety. This paper's central finding is that the organization of obstetric time deserves a place beside the relative procedure fee in the economics of cesarean delivery.
