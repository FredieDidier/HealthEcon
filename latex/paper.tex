\begin{abstract}
\noindent Brazil's private sector delivers about 80\% of babies by cesarean, the
highest rate documented for any large health system. Combining the universe of
private insurance claims with birth records, I show the epidemic is not
financial: counting the hourly labor-assistance fee, cesareans pay physicians
less than vaginal deliveries in the largest states, and rates do not respond to
fee variation. The cause is physician time. Cesareans cluster on weekdays and
business hours and fall on weekends and holidays, entirely through deliveries
scheduled before labor. The pattern holds among low-risk mothers and shifts
births earlier. A model of physician time rationalizes these facts.
\end{abstract}

\bigskip
\noindent \textbf{JEL codes:} I11, I13, J22, D82. \\
\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} cesarean section, physician agency, supplier-induced
demand, physician time, Brazil.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Cesarean section is the most common major surgery in the world, and its use has
risen far beyond any clinical benchmark. About 21\% of births worldwide are
cesarean deliveries, a share projected to reach nearly 29\% by 2030
\citep{betran2021}, against a World Health Organization reference of 10--15\%,
the range above which population-level gains in maternal and newborn survival
disappear \citep{who2015cesarean}. Overuse is not innocuous: unnecessary
cesareans raise maternal morbidity, respiratory distress and other neonatal
complications, and costs \citep{sandall2018,boerma2018}. Brazil is the frontier
case. Roughly 57\% of all Brazilian births are cesarean, and in the private
insurance sector, which covers about a quarter of the population, the rate
reaches 80\%, the highest documented for any large health system. The excess is
not confined to complicated pregnancies. Among first-time mothers at term with a
single, well-positioned baby who are already in spontaneous labor, the group for
which surgery is hardest to justify on clinical grounds, two thirds of private
weekday deliveries end in a cesarean, against roughly one third in public
hospitals. Understanding what sustains surgical rates of this magnitude in a
modern, well-financed health sector is a first-order question for health policy
well beyond Brazil.

Economic theory offers a natural suspect and a large literature to back it. When
patients cannot judge treatment necessity, physicians act as imperfect agents
and can tilt clinical decisions toward their own interests \citep{mcguire2000}.
The canonical version of this argument is financial: providers do more of what
pays more. \citet{gruber1996physician} show that obstetricians substitute toward
cesareans when fee differentials widen, \citet{gruber1999physician} confirm the
fee margin in Medicaid data, and \citet{clemens2014} document strong treatment
responses to payment changes in Medicare. Childbirth has become the laboratory
of choice for testing physician agency, precisely because the cesarean decision
is frequent, discretionary, and well measured: \citet{currie2008} show that
liability pressure distorts delivery mode, \citet{johnson2016} find that
physicians treat informed patients (other physicians) differently from everyone
else, and \citet{card2023} show that being delivered in a hospital with a high
cesarean propensity harms the marginal newborn. Throughout this literature the
operative margin is money. Yet the physician's scarcest input is not billable
revenue but time. A vaginal delivery is a long commitment of uncertain onset and
duration that blocks nights, weekends, and office hours; a scheduled cesarean is
a one-hour appointment at a time of the physician's choosing. Separating this
time-and-convenience channel from the fee channel requires observing actual
physician fees and the exact timing of births in the same setting, a combination
administrative data rarely provide.

This paper separates the two channels at national scale. I combine the universe
of hospital claims in Brazil's private insurance system (TISS), which records
the physician fee actually billed for every delivery from 2015 to 2025, with the
universe of birth records (SINASC), 42 million births from 2010 to 2024 with the
exact date and hour of birth, the Robson clinical classification, and an
indicator for whether a cesarean was performed before labor began. The claims
data measure the money incentive; the birth records reveal the time incentive
through the calendar. Three sets of facts emerge.

First, the epidemic is not a price story. The correct financial comparison must
credit the vaginal delivery with the separately billed hourly labor-assistance
fee, which averages 2.7 hours at roughly R\$409 per hour. Once it is counted, a
cesarean pays the physician 10 to 30\% less than a vaginal delivery in Brazil's
largest states, which nonetheless run cesarean rates near 80\%. Across
municipalities and years, the association between the fee gap and the cesarean
rate is tiny and flips sign across specifications. State-level fee swings as
large as two log points leave cesarean rates unchanged. Even a 2015 federal
court order directing the national regulator to make insurers pay at least three
times more for vaginal deliveries produced no movement in fees or in cesarean
rates. Money does not explain where, when, or how often Brazilian physicians
operate.

Second, the calendar does. Because the onset of spontaneous labor does not
respect the week, the day on which a birth falls is as good as random with
respect to medical need, and any systematic weekly pattern in surgery reveals
choices about timing. Cesarean rates in the private sector fall by 8.3
percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points on national holidays, roughly twice
the public-sector dips, and half of all cesareans are performed in weekday
business hours, against a 30\% benchmark under uniform timing. The mechanism is
visible in its components: the weekend dip is minus 9.7 percentage points for
cesareans scheduled before labor and plus 1.7 points for cesareans during labor,
so the entire pattern comes from surgery booked around the physician's calendar,
with a small displacement of unscheduled women into in-labor surgery. The
pattern persists among low-risk first-time mothers in spontaneous labor, is 2.2
points larger in municipalities where obstetricians are scarce relative to
births, and is no stronger for educated mothers, which points to the supply
side rather than to maternal demand for convenient timing.

Third, convenience has a quantity and a cost. A decomposition over Robson
clinical groups attributes 71\% of the 35 percentage-point private-public gap to
practice style within identical clinical groups, not to differences in case-mix.
Benchmarking each municipality's weekday cesarean propensity against its own
weekend rate implies about 50,000 excess weekday cesareans per year in the
private sector alone, about one in ten private cesareans. Scheduling also moves
births earlier: private births are 10.9 percentage points more likely to occur
at 37--38 weeks of gestation than public births after controlling for maternal
age, education, and race, and prelabor cesareans mass exactly at those weeks.
Early-term delivery is precisely the margin that the clinical literature links
to higher neonatal respiratory morbidity and intensive-care admission
\citep{tita2009}, and quasi-experimental evidence confirms that non-indicated
cesareans harm newborn health \citep{costaramon2018,card2023}.

A simple model of physician time allocation rationalizes all of these facts jointly. A physician
chooses between attending a vaginal delivery, which arrives at a random hour,
lasts long, and blocks the calendar, and scheduling a one-hour cesarean at the
time she prefers. The choice depends on the fee gap plus a convenience premium:
the value of converting an unschedulable liability into a business-hours
appointment. The premium is positive even where the fee gap is negative, which
is exactly the configuration the data show. The model delivers the fee
irrelevance, the weekday and business-hour clustering of prelabor cesareans, the
larger dips where obstetric time is scarce, and the early-term shift as a single
package, and it explains why fee-based policy levers have no traction: they act
on the wrong wedge.

The policy record is consistent with this reading. Beyond the toothless court
order, I evaluate Parto Adequado, the flagship national quality program run by
the regulator with participating hospitals from 2015 onward. Municipalities
hosting participating private hospitals do show falling cesarean rates, but the
decline starts in 2010, seven years before the program's dissemination phase,
and shows no break at adoption; an event study with a long pre-period rejects
parallel trends, and controlling for the pre-existing trend eliminates the
estimated effect entirely. The decline is real, but its attribution to the
program is not supported. If the binding constraint is the physician's time,
the relevant policy class is organizational: on-call and shift coverage,
midwife-led continuity of care, and hospital scheduling norms, rather than fee
schedules.

The paper contributes to three literatures. The first is the economics of
physician agency and supplier-induced demand \citep{mcguire2000,%
gruber1996physician,gruber1999physician,clemens2014}. That literature has
established that providers respond to financial incentives, and the canonical
test is whether treatment intensity tracks fees. I bring a setting where the
financial channel can be measured directly, through actual billed fees, and
rejected: cesarean use does not track the fee gap, and it is high precisely
where cesareans pay less. In its place I identify and measure a distinct
channel, the opportunity cost of the physician's own time, and show that agency
operates through the calendar rather than the fee schedule. This reframes
supplier-induced demand around a non-price margin that the incentive literature
has largely left implicit.

The second is the economics of childbirth, where the cesarean decision has
become a workhorse for studying physician behavior \citep{currie2008,%
johnson2016,costaramon2018,card2023}. \citet{johnson2016} use information
asymmetry between doctor and patient, and \citet{currie2008} use liability
pressure, to move the delivery-mode margin; \citet{card2023} trace its
consequences for newborn health. I contribute the first national anatomy of the
most extreme cesarean system on record, assembled from the universe of private
claims and birth records, and a portable set of scheduling fingerprints,
weekend and holiday dips, the business-hour peak, and the prelabor-versus-labor
split, that can be reproduced wherever birth records carry a date.

The third is the design of health policy in middle-income countries. A decade of
Brazilian interventions aimed the fee schedule and mandatory disclosure at the
epidemic and did not move it. I show why: a court order to triple the vaginal fee
never changed relative pay, and the flagship quality program tracked a pre-existing
municipal trend rather than causing it. The evidence redirects attention from
prices to the organization of obstetric time, the on-call coverage and scheduling
norms that determine the convenience premium, as the margin worth regulating.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
describes the private health sector, delivery payment, and the policy
environment. Section~\ref{sec:data} presents the two administrative datasets and
the construction of the analysis files. Section~\ref{sec:notprice} shows that
fees do not explain the epidemic. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} presents the
scheduling evidence and its checks. Section~\ref{sec:cost} quantifies the
epidemic's composition and its health margin. Section~\ref{sec:policy} evaluates
the policy record, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
Appendix~\ref{sec:model} develops the model.

\section{Institutional background}\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================

About a quarter of Brazilians hold private health insurance, a parallel system
of insurers (\emph{operadoras}) and accredited hospitals regulated by the
national agency ANS. The rest rely on the public single-payer system (SUS).
Childbirth is covered by both, but the two sectors deliver very differently.
Figure~\ref{fig:trend} plots the cesarean share of births by sector over time.
In the private sector the rate sits near 80\% throughout, whether measured from
insurance claims or from birth records at for-profit hospitals; in the public
sector it is close to 44\%. Both are far above the 10--15\% range the World
Health Organization uses as a reference.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{../analysis/output/graphs/fig01_csection_trend.pdf}
\caption{Cesarean rate by sector, 2010--2024 \textit{Notes:}  Cesarean share of deliveries. ``Private (TISS claims)'' is the
insurance-financed sector; ``Private/Public (SINASC births)'' classify births by
the legal nature of the establishment (for-profit vs public).
}\label{fig:trend}
\end{figure}

Private obstetricians are paid fee-for-service on a procedure schedule. A
delivery generates a fee for the procedure itself, and a vaginal delivery
additionally generates an hourly ``labor assistance'' fee for time spent
attending labor, billed up to six hours. This detail matters for measuring
incentives and is central to Section~\ref{sec:notprice}.

Three policy episodes recur in the paper, and their institutional scope is worth
stating precisely. Resolution RN 368/2015 is a national ANS rule, binding on all
private plans, that created a right to operator-, hospital-, and
physician-specific cesarean rates on request and made the partogram and an
informed-consent term mandatory for elective cesareans. In December 2015 the
Federal Court in S\~ao Paulo, ruling on a civil action by the state prosecutor,
ordered the ANS, a national regulator, to issue rules making plans pay at least
three times more for vaginal than for cesarean delivery; the agency appealed, and
the rule never took effect. Parto Adequado is a national quality program run by
the ANS with a private hospital and the Institute for Healthcare Improvement;
adoption is voluntary and at the hospital level, with a 2015--16 pilot and a
2017--2021 dissemination phase of about 113 hospitals in 60 municipalities.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
% =============================================================================

The analysis combines two national administrative universes. The first is the
TISS hospital claims that private insurers report to the ANS, covering every
insurance-financed delivery from 2015 to 2025. Each claim records the
beneficiary's age band and municipality, the provider's municipality, the
diagnosis, and, at the procedure level, the fee billed. I identify deliveries by
their procedure codes and classify each as cesarean or vaginal, and I measure the
physician fee for each mode. The second is SINASC, the birth registry, with 42
million births from 2010 to 2024. SINASC records the exact date and hour of
birth, the Robson clinical group, gestational age, and an indicator for whether a
cesarean was performed before labor began, and it assigns each birth to an
establishment whose sector I take from the national facility registry (CNES).
I supplement these with obstetrician counts from the CNES professionals file and
with municipal covariates from IEPS.

From these sources I build two files. The first is a municipality-year panel of
private deliveries from the claims, with the cesarean rate, the two fees, and
municipal covariates, used for the fee analysis. The second is a
municipality-by-date file of births by sector from the registry, used for the
timing analysis. Table~\ref{tab:descriptives} reports summary statistics for the
municipality-year panel. The full variable codebook is provided as a
supplementary spreadsheet, and Appendix~\ref{app:data} documents the
construction.

\section{The epidemic is not a price story}\label{sec:notprice}
% =============================================================================

The natural explanation for a high cesarean rate is that cesareans pay more. The
correct comparison, however, must credit the vaginal delivery with the hourly
labor-assistance fee it generates, which averages 2.7 hours at about R\$409 per
hour. Once it is counted, the \emph{economic} cesarean-minus-vaginal fee gap is
negative in Brazil's largest states, by 10 to 30 percent, even though those
states run cesarean rates near 80 percent. The raw correlation already points
away from the price story.

Table~\ref{tab:not_a_price} makes this precise. It regresses the private cesarean
rate on the log economic fee gap across municipality-years, weighting by
deliveries and adding state or municipality fixed effects and controls for
income, plan coverage, prenatal care, and obstetrician density. The coefficient
is small and changes sign across specifications: it is a positive 0.017 with
state fixed effects and a negative 0.014 with municipality fixed effects, and
never significant. A fee gap that swings by a full log point moves the cesarean
rate by less than two percentage points, with an interval that includes zero.

\input{../analysis/output/tables/tab02_not_a_price.tex}

Fees do move over time, and Table~\ref{tab:fee_shock} exploits that variation
directly. State-level fee gaps swing by as much as two log points from one year
to the next, yet cesarean rates do not follow. In first differences the
coefficient is a precisely estimated near-zero with the wrong sign, and in levels
with state and year fixed effects it is insignificant. Figure~\ref{fig:fee_shock}
shows the same result graphically: the change in the cesarean rate is flat in the
change in the fee gap. Consistent with all of this, the 2015 court order to
triple vaginal pay produced no jump in the fee series and no change in the
cesarean rate. Money does not explain the epidemic.

\section{The convenience mechanism}\label{sec:mechanism}
% =============================================================================

If not money, then time. The onset of spontaneous labor does not respect the
calendar, so the day on which a birth falls is as good as random with respect to
medical need. Any systematic weekly pattern in the mode of delivery therefore
reveals a choice about timing rather than a difference in the patients who give
birth on different days. Figure~\ref{fig:dow} plots the cesarean rate by day of
the week and sector. In the private sector the rate is flat at about 80 percent
Monday through Friday and drops sharply on Saturday and Sunday. The public
sector shows the same shape at a lower level, and the for-profit and nonprofit
sectors bracket it.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../analysis/output/graphs/fig02_dow_cesarean.pdf}
\caption{Cesarean rate by day of week and sector \textit{Notes:}  SINASC 2010--2024, all births, by establishment sector.
}\label{fig:dow}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:scheduling} estimates the dips in municipality-by-date cells with
municipality and year fixed effects, two-way clustered by municipality and date.
Private cesarean rates fall by 8.3 percentage points on weekends and 5.3 points
on national holidays, each about twice the public-sector dip. The effect is not a
staffing artifact. Figure~\ref{fig:hours} shows that private cesareans are absent
overnight and spike between 8 and 11 in the morning, the operating-room block,
while vaginal births are nearly uniform around the clock. Half of all cesareans
occur in weekday business hours, against a benchmark of 29.8 percent under
uniform timing, and only about a third of vaginal births do.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{../analysis/output/graphs/fig07_hour_of_birth.pdf}
\caption{Hour-of-birth distribution, by delivery mode and sector \textit{Notes:}  SINASC 2010--2024, share of births by hour. Cesareans peak in the
morning operating-room block; vaginal births are near-uniform.
}\label{fig:hours}
\end{figure}

The decisive evidence is in the components of the cesarean rate.
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks} splits it into cesareans performed before labor
began and cesareans performed during labor. In the private sector the weekend
dip is 9.7 percentage points for prelabor cesareans and a positive 1.7 points for
in-labor cesareans. Scheduling can only operate on prelabor surgery, and that is
exactly where the entire dip sits; the small positive in-labor term is
displacement, as women who are not scheduled go into labor and some convert. A
placebo confirms the reading: preterm births (Robson group 10), which cannot be
freely scheduled, dip far less. Daily counts, in Figure~\ref{fig:daily_counts},
show cesarean volumes collapsing on weekends while vaginal volumes barely move,
which rules out a compositional change in who is admitted.

vaginal counts are stable.
}\label{fig:daily_counts}
\end{figure}

The pattern is not confined to complicated pregnancies. Figure~\ref{fig:robson_dow}
and Table~\ref{tab:robson} restrict attention to Robson groups 1 and 2, first-time
mothers at term with a single cephalic baby, the births for which a cesarean is
hardest to justify. The private weekday rate still exceeds 70 percent and the
weekend dip is 7.4 percentage points, against 5.0 in the public sector. Among
Robson group 1 alone, women in spontaneous labor, the private dip is 6.5 points.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:permutation} pushes this further with exact randomization
inference: across all twenty-one ways of choosing two days of the week as ``rest
days,'' the true weekend produces the largest dip, an exact permutation p-value of
0.048.

points larger in municipalities where obstetricians are scarce relative to
births, exactly where the opportunity cost of an unschedulable delivery is
highest. And the dip is no larger, if anything smaller, for more-educated
mothers, which is hard to square with a story in which mothers themselves demand
convenient timing. Physician-owned cooperative operators use cesareans slightly

though the difference is not significant.

\section{The cost of convenience}\label{sec:cost}
% =============================================================================

Scheduling a cesarean means performing it before labor, and to be sure of
preceding labor the physician must book it before the natural rise in the
delivery hazard around week 39. The mechanical consequence is a shift of births
to earlier gestational ages. Figure~\ref{fig:gestation} shows that private births
mass at 37 and 38 weeks where public births mass at 39 and 40, and the shift is
carried entirely by prelabor cesareans (Figure~\ref{fig:gestation_timing}).
Table~\ref{tab:health} estimates that private births are 10.9 percentage points
more likely to be early-term (37--38 weeks) than public births, after controlling
for maternal age, education, and race with municipality and year fixed effects.
Early-term delivery is the margin the clinical literature links to higher
neonatal respiratory morbidity and intensive-care use \citep{tita2009}, and
quasi-experimental work confirms that non-indicated and early cesareans harm
newborn health \citep{costaramon2018,card2023}. Private births show lower
low-birthweight and low-Apgar rates, which reflects the better resources and
healthier mothers of the private sector; I report these honestly, and the
health margin the scheduling mechanism produces is the shift to early term.

How large is the convenience margin? Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} decomposes the
35 percentage-point private-public gap over Robson clinical groups. Only 29
percent reflects differences in case-mix; the remaining 71 percent is
within-group practice style, the same women managed differently. Benchmarking
each municipality's weekday cesarean propensity against its own weekend rate, a
lower bound, implies about 50,000 excess weekday cesareans per year in the
private sector, roughly one in ten private cesareans. Consistent with the
picture that most private cesareans are elective, about 88 to 91 percent carry no
recorded clinical indication in the claims, only a delivery-outcome code
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:no_indication}).

\section{The policy record}\label{sec:policy}
% =============================================================================

The model implies that levers acting on fees should have little traction, and the
Brazilian policy record bears this out. The 2015 court order to triple vaginal
pay never became an actual fee change, as Section~\ref{sec:notprice} showed. The
flagship quality program, Parto Adequado, invites a cleaner test, because
hospitals joined it from 2017. Figure~\ref{fig:pa_es} presents an event study for
municipalities hosting a participating private hospital, using the long
pre-period the birth records afford. The treated municipalities are indeed on a
downward path, but the path begins in 2010, seven years before the dissemination
phase, and shows no break at adoption. A joint test rejects parallel pre-trends,
and controlling for a treated-specific linear trend removes the estimated effect
entirely (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pretrend_robustness}). The decline is real; its
attribution to the program is not supported by the data, a caution for the
short-window evaluations that have credited it. Figure~\ref{fig:rn368} places the
national series in its policy timeline for reference.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

Brazil's private sector performs cesareans at a rate unmatched by any large
health system, and the standard economic explanation does not fit. Measured with
the actual fees, cesareans do not pay more than vaginal deliveries where the
epidemic is worst, and cesarean rates do not respond to fee variation. What the
data reveal instead is a scheduling technology. The cesarean converts an
unschedulable, long, calendar-blocking event into a one-hour appointment, and its
use bears every fingerprint of that logic: it clusters on weekdays and in
business hours, it dips on weekends and holidays, the dip lives entirely in
surgery booked before labor, it persists among the lowest-risk mothers, and it is
larger where physician time is scarcest. The convenience is paid for in earlier
births. This reframes supplier-induced demand around the opportunity cost of the
physician's time, and it implies that the policies likely to move the epidemic are
organizational rather than financial: on-call and shift coverage that detaches the
delivery from the attending physician's calendar, continuity models led by
midwives, and scheduling norms. Fee schedules have been tried, and the wedge they
target is not the one that binds.

\section{Data and variable construction}\label{app:data}
% =============================================================================
This appendix documents the construction of the analysis files; the full
variable codebook is provided as a supplementary spreadsheet. Deliveries in the
TISS claims are identified by their TUSS procedure codes (cesarean 31309054 and
31309208; vaginal 31309127), and the economic vaginal fee adds the
separately-billed hourly labor-assistance code (31309038). In SINASC, the sector
of a birth is the legal nature of the establishment CNES: for-profit
private (2xxx), nonprofit (3xxx), and public (1xxx); the headline private-public
contrast compares for-profit private with public. Because a TISS event can bundle
